It was a dark and stormy night

Everyone knows the line, and almost nobody knows the rest of it. Edward Bulwer-Lytton opened Paul Clifford with it in 1830, and the sentence keeps going for another fifty words of rain and wind and London streets before it lets you go. It's the stock example of a bad opening now, with a contest in its author's name for the worst first sentence anyone can write. The night was never the problem. A first line sits on top of everything the writer already knows: who's in the story, what they want, where it happens, and what it owes the reader by the end.

v1.7 is the Letters and Pipes update. Letters is for the part under the first line: Fabula, for planning the stories set in your world, and the tabula, a board for the thinking that doesn't fit an entry. Pipes is everything underneath that keeps what you've written where you put it: saving, backups, links that survive, a filter that finds things, and a way to move entries in and out.

Fabula

Fabula is a view for planning stories, with the writing itself done somewhere else. A world holds any number of stories, and a corpus gathers the ones that belong together: a series, a shared setting, an anthology. Each story has seven tabs. Story holds the premise and the folding panels around it. People is the cast, their arcs, and their relationships. Plot is the threads, the plot points under your structure's slots, and the promises the story makes, with any it never pays off flagged. Outline is acts, chapters, scenes, and each scene's beats, as one tree you build in place by title. Meaning is the themes and motifs and where each one runs. Notes is research, and what it's used in. Timeline draws the scenes three ways: Chrono, in story time; Narratio, in telling order; and Anachronia, both at once, so the flashbacks show.

Fabula's records aren't entries. They live in their own part of the save file and point outward at your world: a cast record at a Character, a scene at its setting, a plot point at its threads. Plot Thread and Theme / Motif are Fabula's now, still entries, listed from Fabula's index. Each of Fabula's eleven record kinds has a schema the template builder edits, and the story bible exports a story or a whole corpus as HTML or Markdown.

Stemma, twice

Stemma was a radial tree. It still is, as the rota, rebuilt with undo, a keyboard walk, reordering, zoom, and linked nodes that can point at any record in the world. Beside it is the tabula: a board without edges, for the brainstorm that isn't a tree. Notes, links to records, images, columns with lanes, boards inside boards, and lines drawn between cards whose ends stick to what they land on. Both export to Markdown for reading.

Verba volant, scripta manent

Spoken words fly away; written ones stay. The proverb is usually read as advice to put it in writing, and a worldbuilder lives inside it. The world is mostly writing, and the writing is only worth as much as its staying put: the save that lands, the link that still leads somewhere after you rename what it pointed at, the backup that's there when the browser forgets.

Saving you can count on

A change is written a quarter second after you stop typing. A Save writes at once. When the page is hidden, whatever is waiting is written then. If a save fails, a banner says so and keeps trying, and Export now is right there. Cartulary asks the browser to keep its storage, and Settings tells you what the browser said. With an image folder connected, Cartulary writes backups into it as you edit, the same .json Export writes, the newest ten kept. A reset or an import keeps your restore points. None of this is visible when it works, which is the point.

Links that hold

Every page that something links to says so at the bottom: Referenced by, every record and the fields it links through. Deleting something that has links to it asks first and counts what it will clear. A [[link]] reaches a map, a stemma, a Fabula record, or a language now, as well as an entry. Renaming something that links reach by its old name asks first, and can rewrite every one of those links, or keep the old name as a former name so they still land. Concordance draws from the same index, so it shows links it used to miss.

Finding and moving things

All entries lists the whole world in one place. Filter narrows it by any field, by the field's label across templates, and a filter you like saves as a list in the sidebar. The search reads what the page shows. And a template's entries can leave and come back on their own: Export entries writes them as JSON or as a CSV a spreadsheet opens, and Import entries reads either through one mapping screen that shows what it will do before it does it. An import never empties a field.

And more

A Grid field can take a starting shape, so every new entry's table arrives with its rows and columns. A map line's end sticks to the pin or stamp it lands on and follows it. A multi-select takes values of your own. Chronometric makes room for what it draws and fits its frame. The default calendar is Common Year. The in-app help was rewritten wherever any of this touched it, with a new topic for entries files, and cartulary.world follows the help.

Under the hood

Still one HTML file. The module tree it's built from is 62 files now, with Fabula and Stemma each a folder of their own. Every change in this release had to parse, pass checks written for it, and reassemble against a frozen reference before it went out. The repository at github.com/Drakhanas/Cartulary has all of it.

The rest of the sentence

Nobody quotes the rest of Bulwer-Lytton's sentence; it's mostly weather. The rest of a story is a different matter: the cast, the threads, the promises it has to keep, the places and calendars and maps it happens in. Cartulary can't write your first line. It can keep everything behind it where you put it, connected the way you connected it, and there when you come back.
