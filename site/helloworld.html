What it is

A whole world in one file

A cartulary was the single bound volume a monastery kept to gather every charter, deed, and record that proved what it owned. One book, holding the documented existence of an entire estate. This is that, for a world you made up.

Download one HTML file, open it in a browser, and you have a worldbuilding database. Write your characters, cities, factions, gods, and wars as entries. Tie them together. Lay your history on a timeline, your bloodlines on a family tree, your continents on a map, your months on a calendar that runs by your own rules, your loose ideas on a board, and the stories you'll tell in it down to their scenes. Build a constructed language down to its sound changes if that's your kind of madness. All of it lives in that one file and your own browser, on your own machine.

There is no app to install, no account to make, no website you log into. The file is the program. Open it and it runs.

How it's built

The promises, and why they hold

Most software asks you to trust a promise: we won't raise the price, we won't sell your data, we'll still be here next year. Cartulary's promises are different because they aren't promises. They fall out of how the thing is made, and there's no lever to reverse them.

One file, offline, forever

Everything (the program, the fonts, the interface) is inlined into a single HTML document. Once it's on your drive it needs nothing else: no internet, no server, no dependency that can go dark. Run it from a hard drive, a USB stick, an email attachment you sent yourself. It'll open the same way in ten years, on a plane, in a bunker, long after I've stopped working on it.

Your data never leaves your machine

There is no cloud, no telemetry, no analytics, no account. Your world is saved in your browser's own IndexedDB and in the .json files you export. Nothing is uploaded, because there is nowhere to upload it to. There is no "us" sitting on a server with a copy of your world. We could not look at your data if we wanted to.

Free, and it doesn't need me

Cartulary is MIT-licensed, and an MIT license can't be revoked on code that's already shipped. There's no server to meter, no account to gate, and no subscription to sell, because there's no backend at all. What no license can promise is that fixes keep coming. This is one person's project with no release schedule, and nobody can guarantee ten years of updates, me included. So it's built not to depend on me. The source is public on GitHub, the build runs offline from pinned copies of everything it uses, and rebuilding from it gives you the file I ship, byte for byte. If I stop, anyone can pick it up from where I left off.

Built with AI, and saying so

It would be dishonest not to tell you: Cartulary is written with heavy AI assistance. The design, the scope, every feature decision, and all of the testing are mine. The code itself is written by an AI model working under my direction; I decide what gets built and whether it's right, and the model does the typing. On the REAL Rating, a disclosure scale from the nonprofit Real Good AI that runs from REAL 0 (no AI) to REAL 5 (fully AI-made), that's a REAL 4: AI doing substantial production work under human direction. I'm telling you plainly because you're about to trust this file with work you care about, and you deserve to know how it was made. The source is right there inside the file, readable, if you'd rather check than take my word.

Getting started

From empty file to first entry

  1. Open the file. Double-click the downloaded HTML, or drag it into a browser tab. It opens straight to an empty world. Nothing to set up.
  2. Name your world. It starts as Untitled World. Rename it to whatever you're building.
  3. Make an entry. Pick a template (Character, Location, Faction, and a couple dozen others) and create your first record. Fill in as much or as little as you like; an entry just needs a name, though a template can mark fields required (Location, for one, asks for a Type and a Subtype).
  4. Export to save for real. Cartulary autosaves to your browser as you work, but that copy is fragile: it's tied to one browser on one machine and a cache wipe can take it. Your true save is Export .json. A dated .json file is your backup and your version history at once.
  5. Move it anywhere. Import that .json into Cartulary on another machine or browser and your world comes with it. The file travels; the tool is the same everywhere.
The one habit that matters

There is no undo across sessions and no cloud holding a copy. Export a .json often, and keep the dated ones. That single habit is the difference between a safe world and a lost one. Everything else you can learn as you go.

Core concepts

The whole model, in six words

Worlds hold entries. Entries are shaped by templates. Templates are made of fields. Tags and links connect everything. That's the entire mental model; the rest is detail.

  • World

    The top of the pile: one complete setting, with all its entries. You can keep several worlds and switch between them, but each is its own self-contained thing.

  • Entry

    A single record: one person, one city, one war, one god. The atom of your world. Everything you write is an entry of some kind.

  • Template

    The shape of an entry. Character is a template; every character you make shares its set of fields. Cartulary ships with a couple dozen built-in templates, and you can build your own when none of them fit.

  • Field

    One slot on a template: a name, a date, a paragraph, a dropdown, a link to another entry. Fields are what a template is made of, and you can rearrange or invent them.

  • Tag

    A free label you stick on any entry, regardless of its template. Tags cut across the whole world: mark every entry touched by a war, or everything set in one era, and pull them together later.

  • Link

    A field that points at another entry. Point a character at their home city and the two are joined; that connection then shows up on its own, in the relationship and map and timeline views, without you drawing it twice.

Once those six click, the rest of Cartulary is just tools that read them: views that draw your links as a graph or a family tree, exports that turn your entries into a document, a calendar that understands your dates. Each of those has its own page in this guide.

One limit worth naming up front: Cartulary is single-user. One world is open at a time, in one browser. Two people can't edit the same world simultaneously -- there's no real-time sync, no merge resolution, no comments workflow. The way to collaborate is to pass the .json file back and forth. If that's a dealbreaker for your use case, better to know now.

World settings

Your world, and how you keep it

Most of what you set here applies to the whole world, not a single entry, so I gathered the world-level controls in one place. The thing to hold onto is that your world lives in two places at once: in your browser while you work, and in the files you export. The browser copy is convenience. The file is the one you own. Only one world is open at a time, and you keep others as their own files. The sections below run in the same order as the panel on screen, so whatever you're looking at, you'll find it here in the same spot.

Appearance

Two cosmetic controls. Theme is either Default or High Contrast, where the high-contrast option swaps to a black, white, and yellow scheme for low-vision readability. Font Size runs from Small to Extra Large and resizes the whole interface. The one thing it leaves alone is the family tree, which has its own zoom. Set both to whatever you can read comfortably across a long session. Neither touches your data.

World

World Name and Description are labels for the world you're in. Name it something you'll recognize later, when you're staring at a folder of exported files and trying to remember which is which, and use the description for a line of context if you want one. Neither does anything mechanical.

Variant Filter

This is plumbing for constructed-language varieties, and only matters if you're using Glossopoeia's variety system. If that means nothing to you, leave it alone. It's covered on the Glossopoeia page.

Calendar

This section points at your world's calendars, which you actually build over in Kalendarium. What's worth knowing here is the current moment: the date your world treats as "now." You set it from a panel at the top of Kalendarium, and once it's set, a few features wake up. Living characters with a lifespan get their ages figured from it. Maps with time-bound pins can show the world as it stood on that date. Ephemeris can snap its sky view to the present. Leave it unset and those features just show everything regardless of when it happens. Building calendars and setting the moment are covered in full on the Kalendarium page.

Images

Images are the one thing that can make a save file balloon. By default, an image you add gets baked into the world as text. That's convenient, but heavy: the file grows by roughly the image's size plus a third, so a world full of art can swell from a couple hundred kilobytes into many megabytes.

Connecting an image folder fixes that. Click Connect Folder, point it at a directory on your drive, and from then on Cartulary writes your images into that folder and keeps only the filename inside the world. The save stays lean no matter how much art you add. Change repoints the folder, Disconnect ends the arrangement.

Browser support is the catch. Chrome and Edge handle this out of the box. Safari and Firefox don't offer the feature at all, so there the option falls back to baking images in and the file grows heavier. Brave is the in-between case: it has the feature but ships it switched off on purpose, for privacy. You can turn it on by going to brave://flags, searching for "File System Access API," setting it to Enabled, and relaunching. Whether you should is your call, not Cartulary's. Brave disabled it deliberately, so weigh that and decide for yourself. Either way, if you have connected a folder, keep your exported .json beside it, since the world stores those filenames and expects to find them there.

Access

Lock to view-only does what it says. It hides the edit, new, and delete controls and disables the calendar editor, so a world can be read but not changed. The lock travels with the file, so if you hand someone a locked world, they get the read-only version too. Unlocking asks for a confirmation, so you won't trip it by accident. It's the right setting for sharing a world you don't want anyone editing, yourself included, on a tired evening.

Save File

This is the part that matters most, because it's how you keep your work at all.

Export world hands you a .json file. That file is the real, canonical save: everything in your world, in a format Cartulary can load back later. Treat it as the thing you own and the thing you back up. If you've linked an image folder, keep the .json next to it.

Two other formats exist for when you want a readable copy rather than a working save. Export as Markdown gives you a .md document of your world, good for reading or pasting elsewhere. Export as Markdown + images bundles that same .md with all its referenced images into a .zip, a self-contained reference you can hand to someone who doesn't use Cartulary. Each comes in a plain version and a "with secrets" version. The secrets version includes any fields you've marked secret, so keep those for your own eyes or your GM notes, not for your players.

Import world loads a .json back in, and this is also how you switch worlds: importing replaces whatever is currently open. So the way you juggle several settings is to keep each as its own exported file and import the one you want. Export the current world first if you haven't, or you'll lose anything changed since your last save. Whole worlds aside, individual templates, packs, conlangs, stemmas, and calendars each export and import on their own; Import & Export maps the lot.

Reminders

Here's the thing I'd underline in red if I could: Cartulary has no single undo button, and nothing in the cloud holding a copy of your work. There's a Version History in Settings that can roll you back within a session, covered just below, but it lives in the same browser as your world and won't survive that storage being wiped. So the durable way back from a bad delete is always an earlier .json. That's the whole reason backups matter.

The Backup reminder is your safety net. With it on, a banner turns up once it's been more than two hours since your last JSON export and you've made changes since, nudging you to save. It tracks JSON exports specifically; a Markdown or zip export doesn't reset it, since neither can be re-imported. Hit Export now for a fresh .json, or Snooze 1h if you're mid-thought. Importing a file also clears the reminder, since the file you just loaded is by definition your most recent backup. Leave this on. The few seconds it costs are nothing next to losing an evening's work.

Version History

This is the net under the wire, and the reason I can talk about deleting things without total dread. Open Settings and Cartulary keeps a Version History of recent states you can roll back to. It works two ways at once. It takes automatic snapshots as you go, a few minutes apart, holding the last several and quietly dropping the oldest. And you can pin your own with Create restore point, which stay put until you remove them and can be labeled, so "Before the big rename" is sitting there waiting when the big rename goes sideways.

Each saved state gives you three choices. Restore swaps your current world for that snapshot, after a confirmation, and it takes a snapshot of the present state first, so even the rollback is reversible. Export downloads that snapshot as its own .json. Delete removes it.

The catch is the one that runs through this whole page: this history lives in the same browser storage as your working world. Anything that wipes that storage, clearing site data, a profile reset, a privacy extension, takes the history with it. Version History is real safety inside a session and worth nothing against losing the browser copy. It's the fast undo; the exported .json is the actual backup. Use both.

Danger Zone

One control sits here, and it throws work away: Start new world, whose button reads Reset. It replaces what's open with a blank world, so export first if you want to keep the current one. Before it wipes, it pins a "Before reset" snapshot into Version History, so a fresh start is reversible within the session; but it also clears the rest of that history, and none of it survives losing the browser copy. The durable way back is a .json you saved earlier, which, one more time, is why you keep them.

Templates

The shapes your entries take

A template is the shape of an entry: the set of fields a Character has, or a Faction, or a Location. Concepts has the one-line version of that idea; this page is the practical one. You get templates two ways. Cartulary ships a built-in set that covers the common ground, and when none of them fit what you're modeling, you build your own. Both kinds behave the same once they exist, and nothing here is precious: every built-in is yours to edit, rename, strip down, or throw away.

The built-in set

The starting set covers a couple dozen common worldbuilding subjects, grouped below by what they're for. I won't print an exact count, because it moves as the tool grows: when a subject gets big enough to deserve its own dedicated view, it graduates out of the plain template list. Calendars, maps, constructed languages, and the night sky all went that way, which is why you won't find a Map template in the sidebar (you work with maps over in Cartographia instead). The live, current list is always the Templates section of the sidebar. What's there is the truth; this is the map of it.

  • People and peoples

    Character, Race / Species / Ancestry, Culture / People, House / Dynasty / Clan / Bloodline, Creature.

  • Powers and beings

    Deity / Entity, Magic / Tech System.

  • Places and cosmos

    Location, Cosmology / Plane.

  • Groups

    Faction / Government / Organization, Religion / Belief System.

  • Things

    Artifact / Object / Currency, Weapon / Armor, Vehicle, Substance / Material, Book / Document / Text / Tome.

  • Events and time

    Historical Event, Festival / Holiday.

  • Story scaffolding

    Plot Thread, Theme / Motif. Since v1.7 these two are Fabula's: still templates with ordinary entries, but listed and edited from Fabula's index, not from the sidebar's Templates list.

Every one of these is a starting point, not a rule. Keeping the set small is a deliberate choice: a tight, strong default you can extend yourself beats a sprawling one nobody asked for. If a built-in is close but not quite right, edit it. If it's useless to you, delete it. If you need something that isn't here at all, build it. The next three sections are those three moves.

Making an entry

Getting started walks the basic flow: pick a template, make a record, fill in what you want. What's worth adding here is that everything you see on that form comes from the template. The fields, their order, which ones sit side by side, which only appear once another field is set a certain way: all of it is the template's doing, not a fixed Cartulary layout. A Character form looks the way it does because the Character template was built that way. Change the template and every Character changes with it.

One rule the forms quietly enforce for you: a link can't point at its own entry. A person can't be their own parent, a faction can't list itself as an ally. The dropdown for a link field leaves the current entry out of its own options, and if an older file somehow carries a self-link, it's cleaned up when the world loads.

Building your own

When the built-ins don't fit, click + New Template in the sidebar. You name it, pick an icon glyph and an accent color, and then you're in the Template Builder, adding the fields the thing actually needs.

Most of the work is just label plus type. A short text for a name, a long text for a description, a dropdown for a fixed set of options, a link to another entry, a date on your own calendar. The full catalog of field types, with what each is good for, lives on the Fields page. Add a field with + Add Field, remove one with the × on its row, and drag the handle to reorder. The order you set is the order entries show.

Past plain fields, the builder lets fields respond to each other. A field can be set to appear only when another holds a certain value, so a Cause of Death only surfaces once Status is Deceased. Two dropdowns can be chained, so picking a broad Type narrows the choices in a Subtype beneath it. A dropdown can offer an Other option that opens a free-text box when none of the canned answers fit, and a multi-select an Other box for answers of your own. And eligible fields can pair two-to-a-row to keep a long form compact. You set these up once on the template, and every entry of that template inherits them. The full mechanics of each are on the Fields page; the point here is that they're the template author's to arrange.

The thing that makes all of this bearable to design is the preview. Click 👁 Template Preview at the top-left of the builder and a second window opens, showing your work-in-progress template as a real entry form. Type into it. Watch the conditional fields appear and vanish, the cascades narrow, the Other box open, the pairs line up, all live as you edit the template behind it. Whatever you type there is throwaway and never touches your world, and the window closes itself when you leave the builder. If you have a second monitor, drag it across and design with the form in full view beside you.

Editing a built-in

Editing a built-in works exactly like building one. Select it in the sidebar, click Edit Template, and the same builder opens, preview button and all. Change whatever you like. The only thing separating a built-in from one you made is where it came from, and that difference shows up in just two places.

The first is Reset to Default, which appears on built-ins only. It puts the template back to how it shipped: factory fields, name, icon, color. It's careful with your data. Fields you edited but kept hold onto their entries, matched by label, so your existing Descriptions survive the reset. Factory fields you'd deleted come back empty. Fields you added yourself are removed, and so is anything stored in them, and the confirmation modal lists exactly what you'll lose before you commit. If the template already matches the factory version, it tells you the reset would do nothing.

The second is Restore Built-ins. Delete a built-in outright and a Restore Built-ins button turns up under + New Template with a count of what's missing. Click it for a checklist of the deleted ones, restore any or all, and they slot back into their old place in the sidebar order. Plot Thread and Theme / Motif aren't in that checklist: a world missing one of them is told so in Fabula, with a Restore button where it's needed. A template you built yourself has neither of these: there's no factory version to reset to, and once deleted it's gone unless you have a JSON backup.

Which is worth saying plainly, because deletion here is heavier than it looks. Deleting a template, built-in or your own, takes every entry made from it along with it, and the delete doesn't snapshot first the way a world reset or import does. You're not without a net: Settings keeps a Version History of recent automatic snapshots plus any restore points you've pinned yourself, so a recent one might still hold what you just deleted. But that history lives in the same browser storage as the world, so treat it as a cushion, not a guarantee. The real safety is a .json you exported beforehand, the same habit the rest of this guide keeps nagging about. Before a big delete, pin a restore point or export.

Sharing a template

A single template can travel between worlds on its own. The Export button on a template opens a small menu with two formats: .template.json, the portable file you hand to someone or carry to another world, and .template.md, a readable reference copy. Import (under + New Template in the sidebar) brings a .template.json back in. If the incoming name collides with a template already in the destination, Cartulary asks what you want: Skip it, Replace the existing one, or import it under a new name so both survive. It never overwrites without asking.

Packs

When you build a set of templates that belong together, a homebrew system, a genre kit, the furniture of one particular setting, you can group them into a pack. A pack is a labeled set that points at templates you've made; it doesn't copy them or wall them off. The templates still live in your one list. The pack just gathers them under a heading in the sidebar.

Built-ins can't be packed, since packs are for your own templates and ones you've imported. Create one with New pack, add templates to it, and a single template can sit in more than one pack at once if it honestly belongs to both. Export pack writes the whole set to a file; Import pack brings someone else's set into your world. Re-import the same pack later and Cartulary reconciles it template by template instead of duplicating, telling you what's new, what's unchanged, and what's changed since last time. Deleting a pack removes only the grouping. The templates and their entries stay put; they just go back to sitting loose in the list.

Templates and packs are two of the things Cartulary can move between worlds. For the whole set, worlds, conlangs, stemmas, calendars and all, see Import & Export.

Fields

The pieces an entry is made of

Every field is two decisions: a label and a type. The label is what you call it. The type decides everything else, what the input looks like, what counts as a valid value, how it stores in the file, and how it renders back on the entry and out across the other views. Templates sent you here for two things: the full catalog of types, and the behaviors that let fields react to each other. This is both. The catalog comes first; the behaviors are at the end.

Text and notes

  • Short Text

    A single line. Names, titles, epithets, a one-line summary. The workhorse, and the type most likely to pair into a side-by-side row with its neighbor (more on that at the end). If you find yourself typing a sentence or two, this is the wrong type. Reach for Long Text.

  • Long Text

    A multi-line box that understands Markdown and wiki-links. This is where descriptions, histories, and notes live. Markdown lets you use headings, bold, italics, and lists, and they render when you read the entry back. Wiki-links mean writing [[Caer Duln]] becomes a live link to that entry, so your prose stitches itself into the rest of the world as you write. Long Text fields always take the full width of the form.

  • Longform Note

    Looks like Long Text but behaves differently on purpose. It keeps your text literal instead of treating it as Markdown, and it gives you two buttons the others don't: Indent, which pushes the current line over by five spaces, and Divider, which drops a page-break line. Use it when the exact spacing matters and you don't want Markdown second-guessing you: a block of verse, a transcribed inscription, an indented outline, anything where the shape on the page is part of the content. The indents and dividers survive a Markdown export intact.

Choices

  • Dropdown

    A single pick from a fixed list of options you define. Status as Living or Deceased, an Allegiance, a Rarity, a Type. Reach for it whenever a field should only ever hold one of a known set of answers, because a clean list beats free text you have to keep spelling the same way. Two of the behaviors at the end of this page build on Dropdowns: the Other escape hatch, and cascades.

  • Multi-select

    A Dropdown that takes more than one answer. Same idea, a fixed set of options, but the field can hold any number of them at once: a creature with several Habitats, a character with multiple Roles. Multi-selects always render full-width. With the Other setting on, a multi-select also takes answers you type, each kept beside the options picked.

  • Tag List

    A field that holds a loose set of short labels you type in, chip by chip, rather than picking from a fixed list. Good for ad-hoc keywords that belong to this one field on this one template. Worth a note: this is a field on a template, separate from the world-wide entry tags that sit at the top of every entry under its name. Those cut across every template and have their own home in the sidebar.

Numbers, marks, and media

  • Number

    A numeric value. Population, weight, a damage figure, a count. Stored as a number, so it behaves like one.

  • Color

    A color value with a swatch picker. For anything where a color is real data: a faction's livery, a banner, the hue a magic school is known by.

  • URL

    A web address that renders as a clickable link when you read the entry. For pointing out of the world: a reference, an inspiration board, a source you're drawing on. Not for linking between entries; that's what wiki-links and Entry Reference are for.

  • Image

    Attaches a picture to the field, either by filename if you've connected a local image folder, or embedded straight into the file if you haven't. The folder-versus-inline tradeoff (a small file with a folder dependency, or a self-contained file that balloons in size) is the Images section of World settings. The Image field is just how a template gets a picture slot in the first place.

Dates

First, the split that trips people up: there are two date worlds here, and they don't mix. A real-world Date is a date in our calendar, for out-of-world bookkeeping. An in-world Calendar Date is a date in your world's own calendar, the one you build in Kalendarium. Use the in-world types for anything that happens in the story; keep the real-world Date for metadata about the entry itself.

  • Date (real-world)

    A standard date picker on the actual Gregorian calendar. For things like when you drafted an entry, a session date, a real publication date for a source. It has nothing to do with your world's timeline.

  • Calendar Date (in-world)

    A single point in your world's time, entered as a year, a month picked from your calendar's own months, and a day bounded by that month's length. A small approximate marker (~) flags dates you're unsure of. Anything carrying a Calendar Date shows up as a point on the Vertical timeline. This is the type for a birth, a founding, the day a war started.

  • Calendar Date Range (in-world)

    A start and an optional end, each able to carry its own approximate flag. Leave the end open and the range reads as ongoing, with an open-ended arrow on the Chronometric timeline; fill both and it renders as a duration bar. A reign, a war, a character's lifespan, an age of the world: anything with extent rather than a single moment. Ranges always render full-width.

Entry Reference

A link from one entry to another. This is the type that turns a pile of entries into a connected world: a character's Liege points at another character, a location's Region points at a larger location, an artifact's Owner points at whoever holds it. Cartulary keeps these links honest in both directions, so a reference you make here turns up as a backlink over in Concordance without you doing a thing.

A few things you set on an entry-ref field when you build it:

  • Single or multiple

    A field can hold one link or many. One Capital city, but many Member factions. Single-value refs can pair side by side with a neighbor; multi-value refs always go full-width.

  • Family role

    You can tag a reference with a family role, Parent of this or Spouse of this, or leave it with none. That tag is what the family tree reads: mark Father, Mother, and the like as Parent refs and the tree assembles itself from them, with no separate tree to draw by hand. Spouse refs can also carry a marriage date and an end date, which the tree and the timelines pick up.

  • What it can point at

    You can scope a ref to one or more templates, so a Spouse field only offers Characters and a Located In field only offers Locations, instead of dumping every entry in the world into the picker. You can narrow further by field value when you need to: a ref to Characters where Status is Living, say. Besides your templates, a ref can point at Astronomical Bodies, the Glossopoeia record types, Maps, and Stemmas, which the builder lists after the templates.

  • It can't point at itself

    A link field never lists its own entry as an option. A person can't be their own parent, a place can't contain itself. If an older file somehow holds a self-link, it gets cleaned out when the world loads.

And the safety net: delete an entry that something else points at, the whole template it belonged to, or a body, a map, or a stemma, and Cartulary clears the dangling links automatically. You're never left with a reference pointing at nothing.

Name History

A record of what an entry used to be called, and when. Each row is a former name plus an optional From and To date; leave From blank for "since the beginning," leave To blank for "still current." New Amsterdam from 1626 to 1664, then New York from 1664 on. A maiden name with a To date at the wedding year. A faction that rebranded twice.

The entry's main Name, up at the top, is always whatever it's called now. Name History tracks everything it was called before, and that history does real work across the world. Wiki-links resolve through it, so [[New Amsterdam]] lands on the New York entry if that's one of its former names, and the link autocomplete suggests former names too, each marked with its current name so you know where you're really pointing. The map, when its time scrubber is on, labels a pin with the name that was current in the scrubbed year: set the year to 1640 and the pin reads New Amsterdam. Both timelines do the same, rendering each event under the name the entry carried in that event's year.

What doesn't shift: the entry's own title, the Concordance, and the family tree all show the current name. Those are "looking back from now" views by design.

A couple of editing notes. Add a row with + Add former name, remove one with its ×. Order doesn't matter, because the system picks the right name by date, not by list position. If two rows claim the same year, usually a mistake, the earlier row quietly wins, so if a timeline label looks wrong, come check here for overlaps. The field doesn't care why a name changed, only what it became and when; the story of the change belongs in a description or a Historical Event.

Repeating Group

A field that holds a list of structured sub-records, each with the same little set of sub-fields. When one value per entry isn't enough and a plain list won't hold the detail, this is the type. A ruler's titles, each with the land it covered and the years held. A ship's notable voyages, each with a date, a destination, and an outcome. You define the shape of one row, and the entry holds as many rows as it needs.

Sub-fields can be almost any type, so a row can mix text, dates, numbers, and links. Groups can even nest, a group inside a group, up to three levels deep, which is enough for org-chart-shaped data without turning the editor into a hall of mirrors. The one type a group can't contain is a Grid, which is heavy enough that it stays top-level only.

Grid / Table

A two-dimensional table, for when your data is genuinely a grid and not a list. You define rows and columns, each with its own label and an optional name for the axis, and every cell holds its own value. The thing that makes Cartulary's grid unusual is that each cell picks its own type, independently: a cell can be plain text, a number, a date or date range on your calendar, or a link to another entry. So one table can hold a label in the first column, a population number in the next, a founding date in the next, and a link to a ruling faction in the last. Cell links can point at anything, an entry or any registered node, with no scoping to set up.

Grids are top-level only: a template can carry as many as you like, but a grid can't sit inside a Repeating Group. Rename or reorder rows and columns freely; the cells stay bound to the right place because the binding is by stable id, not by label or position.

Field behaviors

The catalog is half the story. The other half is what fields can do beyond holding a value: react to each other, gate their own visibility, pair up, and carry a couple of useful flags. The first four below are what Templates pointed you here for; the last two are options you'll reach for less often but should know are there.

  • Show only when

    A field can be set to appear only when another field holds a particular value. A Manner of Death field that stays hidden until Status is set to Deceased; a Capital field that only shows once a Government Type is chosen. Conditions can chain, one field's visibility depending on another that's itself conditional. The form stays uncluttered, showing only what's relevant to what you've entered so far.

  • Cascading dropdowns

    Two Dropdowns can be chained so the second's options depend on the first's pick. Choose a broad Type and the Subtype list narrows to just the subtypes that belong under it: pick Settlement and the Subtype offers City, Town, Village, not Mountain Range. You define the branches once on the template; every entry of it inherits the filtered behavior.

  • The Other option

    A Dropdown can carry an Other choice that, when picked, opens a free-text box for the real value. It's the escape hatch for fields where you want a clean canonical list most of the time but can't swear the list is exhaustive. Canonical options for the common cases, a way out for the one you didn't foresee. A Multi-select can carry it too, as a box under its options: each answer you type there joins the ones you picked.

  • Side-by-side fields

    Short fields pair two-to-a-row automatically to keep forms compact. The eligible types are the narrow ones: Short Text, Number, Date, Calendar Date, Color, URL, Tag List, Dropdown, and single-value Entry Reference. The wide ones, Long Text, Longform Note, Multi-select, multi-value references, Name History, Repeating Groups, Grids, Calendar Date Ranges, and Images, always take a full row. There's no switch to flip. Two eligible fields placed next to each other in the field order pair up; an eligible field with no eligible neighbor stretches to full width on its own. Pairing happens after the show-when rules run, so a hidden field never leaves a gap beside its neighbor.

  • Required

    Mark a field Required and it shows a red asterisk, and trying to save an entry that leaves it empty is refused: you get a message naming what's missing, and the rest of your edits stay put while you fix it. Off by default, and most fields don't need it, but it's there for the one or two a template genuinely can't do without.

  • Secret

    Mark a field Secret and it carries a SECRET tag, drops out of any export you run without secrets, and hides when you switch secrets off while reading. It's how you keep a hidden truth, a twist, a real identity, in the same entry as the public-facing material without it leaking into a copy you hand to players. The export side of this is the Access section of World settings.

    The toggle itself is the small dot icon in the top-right of the app -- ◎ when secrets are hidden, ◉ when they're showing. Its tooltip reads "Show secret fields" or "Hide secret fields." Switching it on and off is ephemeral: it resets to hidden when you reload the page.

The long-text editor

Long Text fields have a small toolbar above the text box. Left to right: B (bold) and I (italic); H and H₃ for a heading and a sub-heading; bullet-list, numbered-list, and quote buttons; [[ ]], which drops empty wiki-link brackets with the cursor inside, ready for autocomplete; ↗, which inserts an external-link template; an image-insert button; and an inline-code button. A Preview toggle on the right swaps the box for the rendered view. Longform Note fields are literal rather than Markdown, so they carry a different two-button toolbar, Indent and Divider, described above, not this one.

Keyboard shortcuts work when focus is in a Long Text field: Ctrl+B for bold, Ctrl+I for italic, Ctrl+K for an external link. Wiki-link autocomplete navigation: arrow keys move through suggestions, Enter or Tab accepts, Esc dismisses.

Search

The search input at the top of each template's entry list filters as you type. It looks at the entry's name, its tags, and the content of every field -- text, long-text, tag fields, dropdowns, all of it. It's scoped to the active template filter, so searching while a template is selected searches within that template only. Deselect the active template first to search across the whole world.

A few things search doesn't cover: template definitions and field labels (it searches entries, not templates), maps (records in Cartographia with their own list, so neither their fields nor their pin labels), and former-name resolution (a search for "New Amsterdam" finds entries that have that text somewhere in a field, but doesn't apply the temporal name-mapping logic that wiki-links use). There's no regex, no advanced query syntax, no field-specific scoping.

Linea

The family tree, drawn for you

Linea is the family tree, and it draws itself. You don't place anyone on a canvas or route a single line. You set who a person's parents and spouse are, on their own entry, and the tree falls out of that. Point Linea at someone and it shows their ancestors climbing up and their descendants spreading down, with that person at the center.

Where it comes from

Linea reads exactly one thing: entry-ref fields whose family role is set to Parent of this or Spouse of this. That's the family-role setting on the Fields page; nothing else feeds the tree. The Character template ships with Father, Mother, and Spouse already wired, so for characters you get a tree for free the moment you fill those in. Set the same family roles on any other template and its entries join the tree too. If Linea looks empty, it's because nothing in view has a parent or spouse reference set yet, not because anything's broken.

Picking the center, and how far it reaches

A family tree only means anything relative to a person, so Linea always has a focal: the one at the center, with everyone else placed in relation to them. Pick the focal from the selector in the toolbar, and changing it recomputes the whole tree around the new center. Two number inputs, Generations up and Generations down, set how far the tree climbs and descends, each from 1 to 10, both starting at 5. Five each way covers most trees. Push them higher only if your genealogy genuinely runs that deep, because a fully populated ten-generation ancestor climb allocates over a thousand slots and gets very wide. A small count below the canvas reports how many ancestors, focal-row people, and descendants the current tree holds.

Reading a node

Each node is a person. The large line is their name, cut off with an ellipsis if it's too long for the box, with the full name on hover. The small italic line beneath is their lifespan, taken from the first Calendar Date Range field on their template. It reads 1903–2003 when both ends are set, 1980–Living when there's no end and the entry isn't marked Deceased, 1980–? when it's marked Deceased with no death year, and ~1742–1751 when either end is approximate, the ~ sitting on whichever end is uncertain. An entry with no lifespan field, or an empty one, shows its template name in that slot instead.

If your world runs more than one calendar, a calendar selector appears in the toolbar. The lifespan years project through whichever calendar you pick, so a tree that pulls together characters dated in different cultures' calendars still reads in one consistent reckoning instead of a jumble of mismatched years.

Status does one more thing. A character whose Status is Deceased renders dimmed, with the name in italics and a ✝ in front. Living and Unknown both look normal; only Deceased gets the mark, and it's independent of marriage status, so a dead former spouse shows both at once.

How it's laid out

Ancestors stack above the focal in Sosa-Stradonitz order, the ahnentafel numbering genealogists use: each generation up has twice the slots of the one below it, the father's line climbing to the left and the mother's to the right. Which parent counts as father and which as mother comes from their labels. A parent whose label includes "Father" or "Mother" takes that side; if neither label says, the sides are handed out by entry ID, which is consistent but arbitrary. If you care which side someone lands on, label them Father or Mother and the question settles itself.

Descendants hang below. When a child has two or more parents shown, the lines don't run independently from each one; they meet at a junction between the rows, drop to a shared horizontal at the parents' midpoint, and a single line continues down to a siblings bar above the children, then splits out to each child. It's the convention the large genealogy sites use, and it reads more cleanly than a tangle of separate lines. A single parent just gets a simple L-shaped line.

Spouses sit next to the focal on the same row. And if a person has more than two parents, a biological pair plus an adoptive one, say, the extra parents aren't dropped from the chart; they're placed in the same generation row beside the two the Sosa positions hold.

Spouses, status, and dates

A Spouse reference on a Character carries more than the link. It has a status, Active, Former, or Estranged, and two optional dates, Married and Ended. All of it shows up in the entry, in the Markdown export, and feeds the Marriages lane on the Chronometric timeline, so a marriage becomes a dated band on the world's timeline rather than just a pairing on the tree.

The two sides can't contradict each other. Declare A's spouse as B and save, and B gets the matching declaration automatically, same status, same dates. Edit one side and the other follows on save; remove one and the partner's side goes with it. You can't end up with a file where A was married in 1746 and B wasn't, because the pair gets reconciled every time you save.

One thing isn't mirrored: the override label. When you set a family reference you can type a label beside it, Adoptive Father, Stepmother, Estranged Wife, and that label is yours alone, because each side of a relationship names it from their own vantage. It shows on the entry's chips and in the Markdown export. Linea itself doesn't print the override on the canvas; the shape of the tree already says what the relationship is, so the label would only add clutter.

Getting around, and getting a tree out

The canvas zooms and pans: scroll to zoom, anywhere from a quarter size up to two and a half times, and drag empty space to move around. Clicking a node recenters the tree on that person, making them the new focal. To jump from a node to the entry behind it instead, hold Shift and click. And from the focal panel you can export the current tree as Markdown: the focal person plus everyone reachable from them, numbered by the same Sosa-Stradonitz scheme, ready to drop into notes or hand to someone else.

Fabula

Everything but the writing

Fabula is the view for planning the stories set in your world: each story's premise, cast, plot, outline down to the beats of every scene, themes, and research, with the writing itself done somewhere else. A world holds any number of stories, and a corpus gathers the ones that belong together: a series, a shared setting, an anthology. Fabula's records aren't entries. They live in their own part of the world (fabula in the save file; see JSON Structure), point outward at your entries, and travel with the world's .json. Two built-in templates belong to it: Plot Thread and Theme / Motif stay entries, but Fabula's index lists and edits them, not the sidebar.

Your first story

  1. Make the story. Click Fabula in the sidebar, then + New Story. The new row opens on its title: type one and press Enter. Click the row to open the story's page.
  2. Give it a structure. On the Story tab, click Edit and pick a Structure: Three-Act, Freytag, Hero's Journey, Seven-Point, Kishotenketsu, Custom (your own slots, one per row), or None. Its turning points become the Plot tab's headings. Fill in what you know of the Premise, Genre, Narration, and Setting panels; every field is optional. Save.
  3. Cast it. On People, click + New Cast for each character in the story. A cast record is an entry's part in this story: pick its Character (a Character or a Deity / Entity entry), its Importance, and its Role. One cast record per character per story.
  4. Outline it. On Outline, add acts with + Part / Act, chapters inside an act with its + Chapter, and scenes with + Scene. Each takes its title in place; Enter adds it. Under a scene, + Beat adds a beat: the beat in a line, Tab to What Happens, Enter keeps it. Positions (I, I.1, I.1.A) come from where things sit.
  5. Plot it. On Plot, fill your structure's slots with + Fill this slot; each filled slot is a plot point. Add the story's threads with + Add a thread... or + New Thread, and its promises with + New Promise.
  6. Place a scene. Open a scene (click its row) and Edit it: its POV, Setting, When, Participants, Threads, and Motifs. Its When places it on the Timeline tab.
  7. Say what it means. On Meaning, add the story's themes; on Notes, its research.
  8. Take the bible. Click Export ↓ at the top of the story's page for the story bible, as HTML or as Markdown.

That's the loop. The rest of the view is under the tiles above, part by part: the index and the two kinds of page, People and Plot, the outline and its beats, Meaning and Notes with the two templates Fabula keeps, the timeline, and the Templates section with the bible.

Fabula · Stories and corpora

The index and the two pages

The index

Clicking Fabula opens the index: every corpus with its stories in reading order, then the stories in no corpus, both by name. + New Story adds a story and opens its title for typing; + New Corpus opens a new corpus's page in edit. Each story's row has a Corpus dropdown (No corpus, or any corpus the world has), Rename, and Delete, which asks first and says what goes with the story: every cast record, arc, scene, and other record of its own. A click anywhere else on the row opens the story.

Below the stories, the index lists the world's Plot Threads and its Themes & Motifs, each entry with the stories that use it, with + New Entry, Edit Template, and Export ↓ for the template (see Meaning, Notes, and themes); then Templates, Fabula's own eleven record kinds, each with Edit Template (see Templates and the bible).

A corpus's page

Its Reading order first: the stories in the order the corpus tells them, each with arrows to move it earlier or later, or drag a row; + New Story adds a story to the corpus, Rename renames one in place. Under it, the corpus's fields (Kind, Status, Description, Notes). Edit opens the fields, Delete removes the corpus and leaves its stories in Fabula, in no corpus. Export ↓ writes the corpus's bible, every story in reading order (see the story bible). Deleting a corpus never deletes a story.

The story page

The story's title, then its tabs: Story, People, Plot, Outline, Meaning, Notes, and Timeline. Edit and Delete sit in the header with ← Back, and Export ↓ beside the title on every tab.

The Story tab is the story's own record: Status, Form, Audience, Target Length, Structure (with Custom Slots when the structure is Custom), Threads, Brainstorm (the stemmas behind the story; a chip opens one), and Notes, then four panels that fold under their headings. Premise holds Why, What If, Logline, Theme Statement, Themes, Central Conflict, Conflict Kinds, Stakes, and Synopsis. Genre holds Genre, Subgenre, Tone, Reader Expectations, Expected Moments, and Comparable Titles. Narration holds Point of View, Tense, Narrative Distance, Reliability, Narrators, POV Plan, and Voice & Style. Setting holds Time Period, Story Timespan, Places, Rules in Play, Arena, and Sensory Palette. Which panels are folded is remembered by your browser, one set for every story, and doesn't travel with the world.

Fabula · People and Plot

Who's in it, and what happens

People

The story's Cast, ordered by Importance (Main, Secondary, Minor) and then by name, each row with its importance and roles; its Arcs, by name, each with whose arc it is, its type, and its status; and its Relationships, by name, each with the pair it joins and its kinds. + New Cast, + New Arc, and + New Relationship make a record and open it for editing; a click on a row opens its page, where Edit, Delete, and ← Back work as an entry's do. A reference to a cast record, from a chip on another record's page or from Concordance, opens it the same way.

Deleting a record never takes another with it: Delete says what points at the record and clears those references. When a world entry is deleted, Fabula's references to it are cleared as entries' are, and a cast record whose character it was stays, marked Character deleted, with its roles, arcs, and relationships intact, for you to repoint with Edit or delete.

Plot

Three sections. Threads edits the Story's Threads in place: + Add a thread... adds any Plot Thread entry, + New Thread makes one by its title and adds it, and × takes one off the story (the entry stays in the world). Any thread the story's scenes, plot points, or promises use that the Story doesn't list shows below the listed ones, marked not in the Story's Threads, with Add to the story.

Plot Points are the structure's slots, each a heading with the plot points under it: + Fill this slot makes the first (named for the slot unless you give it a title), + Plot point adds more, and + Custom turn adds a heading of your own at the end, with its own arrows to move it among the slots. Move a plot point up or down with the arrows on its row, with Alt and the same arrow on a focused row, or by dragging; moving it under another heading changes its Slot. A plot point whose slot belongs to a structure the story no longer uses stays at the end, marked not part of this structure, and one with no slot under No slot.

Promises & Payoffs lists the story's promises in the order their first Setup In scene comes in the outline, those with none after, and flags any set up and never paid off as unpaid, whatever its Status. + New Promise adds one. Plot points and promises open on pages of their own, like cast records.

Fabula · Outline and beats

Acts, chapters, scenes, and their beats

The tree

The story's acts, chapters, and scenes as one tree: the story's own level holds anything, an act holds chapters and scenes, a chapter holds scenes, and a scene holds its beats. + Part / Act, + Chapter, and + Scene at the top add at the story's own level; each act ends with its own + Chapter and + Scene, and each chapter with + Scene. Each takes a title in place: Enter adds it, Esc stops.

Positions come from where records sit and update when they move: acts I, II, III; chapters 1, 2, 3 through the whole story; scenes A, B, C within their chapter, their act, or the story's own level; beats i, ii, iii within their scene. A record's position shows on its page, in every reference to it, and in the pickers that list scenes, which keep the outline's order.

Every row has four arrows, move up, move down, move out a level, and move into the part or chapter above, and Alt with the same arrow does the same on a focused row; drag does it with a pointer. The small triangle at a row's left folds what it holds, an act's chapters, a chapter's scenes, or a scene's beats, and unfolds it again; your browser remembers the folds. A click anywhere else on a row opens the record's page. A scene's page also says whose POV it takes when it has none of its own (its chapter's), and lists the research notes whose Used In names it.

Beats

Beats sit under their scene, edited where they are: + Beat adds one, a click on a beat opens it for typing, Enter keeps it, Tab moves to What Happens (what needs to happen in the beat; Ctrl+Enter keeps it from there), and Esc stops. Beats move with their arrows or by drag, within a scene or into another, and × deletes one. A beat with no line and no What Happens is dropped when you leave it.

Deleting from the outline

Deleting a scene clears the references to it, as any record's delete does. Deleting an act or a chapter that holds anything asks instead: Move up puts what it holds in its place, the default; Delete everything deletes it all.

Fabula · Meaning, Notes, and themes

Themes, research, and the two templates Fabula keeps

Meaning

The Premise panel's Theme Statement heads the tab, read-only. Under it, the Story's Themes, edited in place as the Plot tab's threads are: + Add a theme... adds any Theme / Motif entry, + New Theme makes one by its title, × takes one off the story. Themes group under their entries' Type (Themes, Motifs, Symbols, Leitmotifs, Archetypes, then any type of your own, then those with none), and each row shows the positions of the scenes that carry it in their Motifs, in the outline's order, each opening its scene. A theme the story's scenes carry that the Story doesn't list shows marked not in the Story's Themes, with Add to the story.

Notes

The story's research notes by topic, A to Z, each opening on a page of its own; + New Note adds one. A note whose Open Questions holds any text is flagged open wherever its row appears, and the tab's heading counts them; emptying the field clears the flag. A note's Used In names the scenes that need it, and each of those scenes lists the note on its page.

Plot Threads and Themes & Motifs

These two templates are Fabula's since v1.7, and they're still entries: wiki-links, search, tags, Chronometric, Concordance, and the world's exports read them as before. The sidebar's Templates list leaves them out, and so does Restore Built-ins; the index's two sections list their entries, with + New Entry, Edit Template (the ordinary template builder), and Export ↓ for the template, and a world missing one of the templates is told so, with a Restore button wherever it's needed.

An entry of theirs keeps the ordinary entry page, with a Stories list at the end: every story that lists it or uses it, with the scenes that name it by position and, for a thread, its plot points and promises. A story uses a thread when its Threads or any of its scenes', plot points', or promises' Threads name it, and uses a theme when its Themes or any of its scenes' Motifs name it. No pack can take either template.

Fabula · The timeline

Story time and telling order

A story's Timeline tab draws its scenes on a chart of their own, kept apart from Chronometric: one story's scenes, or every scene of the stories in its corpus. It only shows. Nothing on it edits a scene; a click opens the scene's page, and ← Back returns to the tab. The chart runs the full width of the pane.

The controls

Showing picks the scope, This story or The whole corpus, and shows only when the story is in a corpus. Mode picks what the axes mean: Chrono, Narratio, or Anachronia, below. Lanes picks the grouping, By POV, By thread, or By arc; it's hidden in Anachronia, which draws no lanes. Display in picks the calendar the story time is written in, the primary by default, or any additional calendar; it's hidden in Narratio, which has no story time. In Chrono, Range holds the years from and to that the chart shows; in the other two modes the same row is Zoom. +, −, and Reset zoom in, zoom out, and fit the chart to its scenes again; drag the chart to pan, and Ctrl+scroll (⌘+scroll on a Mac) zooms around the pointer. Your picks are kept per story in your browser. While secrets are hidden, a When field marked secret takes every scene off Chrono and Anachronia, which say so; Narratio still draws them, with no When in their hover.

Chrono: story time

Each scene sits where its When falls on the calendar's axis, in your calendar or the one picked under Display in. A When with one date, a start alone, an end alone, or the same day twice, is a point; any other is a span, drawn from its start to its end, open-ended when it has no end and open-started when it has no start. Lanes By POV give each cast record that's a scene's POV a lane, in People's order; a scene with no POV of its own takes its chapter's. By thread gives each thread the story's Plot tab lists a lane, in that order, with the thread's Timespan (its template's first date range) as a bar under the lane's scenes, and a click on the bar opens the thread's entry. By arc gives each of the story's arcs a lane, A to Z; a scene is in an arc's lane when the arc's Turning Points name it. A scene in two lanes is drawn in both.

A scene shows its plot points as diamonds and its arcs' turns as rings on it, and hovering a scene names it with its position, its When, its POV, its plot points, and its turns. Scenes with no When wait in a tray under the chart, by story, each opening on a click. At the corpus's scope the stories' scenes share the chart, in reading order, and a character's cast records from different stories share one POV lane.

Narratio: telling order

Every scene of the scope gets an equal slot in the outline's order, dated or not, and is drawn as a block filling its slot in each lane it belongs to, with its marks at its start and its title past them once the slot is wide enough to hold it (zoom in for the titles). The acts are tinted bands over the chart, named on the axis's upper line; at the corpus's scope the stories are. Chapters are dashed lines named on the lower line. There's no tray and no calendar here.

Anachronia: one order against the other

Telling order runs across, as in Narratio, with its bands, and story time runs up the side, earliest at the bottom, with Chronometric's year ticks. A dated scene is a dot at its slot's middle at the height of its When, or a bar from its start up to its end, and the dated scenes are joined in telling order, one line per story. A book told in order climbs; every flashback drops the line, and every jump forward leaps it. Scenes with no When sit as hollow dots on a strip under the plot. No lanes here, and no Lanes picker.

Not exported

The story bible leaves the timeline out; Chronometric's PNG and SVG export is Chronometric's own.

Fabula · Templates and the bible

The eleven kinds, the bible, and the rest of the world

Templates

Fabula's eleven record kinds (Corpus, Story, Cast, Arc, Relationship, Plot Point, Promise & Payoff, Part / Act, Chapter, Scene, Research Note) each have a schema the template builder edits, from Edit Template in the index's Templates section. The fields Fabula itself reads are core, marked Fabula reads this field in the builder: their type, their place, and, where the seed locks them, their options stay as they are, and they can't be removed, but their label, help line, Secret flag, and panel are yours. Every other field is yours entirely: edit it, remove it, or add fields of your own, which can point at the story's own records (a scene, a cast record) as well as at entries. Saving marks the schema edited on the index.

Reset to Default puts the seed back, with a confirmation that lists the seeded fields it brings back, the fields of yours it removes, and how many records hold data in them; that data is deleted with the fields. When a later version of Cartulary changes the seed, a schema nobody has edited follows it, and an edited schema's core fields are brought up to date on load while the rest is left alone. The schemas, and every seeded field of every kind, are on the JSON Structure page.

The story bible

Export ↓ on a story's page, on any tab, or on a corpus's opens a small modal: the format, HTML or Markdown, and Include secret-marked fields, off by default as the world's exports have it. The file is the story in the page's order: the Story's fields and panels, then People (cast, arcs, relationships), Plot (threads, plot points under their slots, promises and payoffs with the unpaid ones marked), Outline (acts, chapters, and scenes nested, each scene's fields and then its beats), Meaning (themes by type, each with the scenes that carry it), and Notes. A corpus's file opens with the corpus's fields and a Stories list, then each story in reading order.

Every record renders through the same renderers as the world's exports, with its kind's schema as its template, so a secret field is left out unless included and marked [SECRET] when it is, and a hidden or empty field is left out. References to the world's entries are written as names; the Plot Thread and Theme / Motif entries the story lists are written with their fields. In the HTML, a reference to a record in the file links to it, and the file carries a table of contents of the sections and the scenes. The timeline isn't exported. Files download as <name>.story.html, <name>.story.md, <name>.corpus.html, or <name>.corpus.md, and none of them can be imported back; the .json is the save. The world's own HTML and Markdown exports leave Fabula's records out.

Elsewhere in Cartulary

Concordance draws Fabula's records as nodes behind one Fabula row in its filter: a corpus to its stories, a story to its threads and themes, a cast record to its character, a scene to its POV, participants, setting, threads, and motifs, and so on. Lock Mode blocks Fabula's edits, and the secrets toggle hides its secret fields, as everywhere else. The entry list's search and the tags in the sidebar cover entries only. Fabula's own picks, the folded panels, the folded outline rows, and the Timeline tab's settings, stay in your browser and don't travel with the world.

Stemma

Trees and boards for the thinking between entries

Stemma is for the thinking that doesn't belong inside a single entry: plot threads, theme webs, lines of succession, decision trees, a wall of loose notes. One word of warning, because the name invites it: this is not a family tree. Linea is the family tree, and it builds itself from your data. Stemma is the other kind of tool, a canvas you arrange by hand, for structure that lives in your head rather than in your entries. It comes in two layouts. A rota is a radial tree: one node at the center, its children around it, and a click on any child re-centers the tree on it. A tabula is a board: cards where you put them, columns they stack in, boards inside boards, and lines drawn between them.

A stemma is a document

Each stemma is a document of its own, not a view onto your entries. Click Stemma in the sidebar and you land on the Stemmas index: every stemma in the world with its name, a badge saying Rota or Tabula, a count (nodes for a rota, cards and columns for a tabula), the date it was last updated, and any description and tags it carries. + New Rota and + New Tabula start one, and Import brings a .stemma.json in. Every row has its own Export, Markdown, and Delete; a locked world hides Delete, the two new buttons, and Import. Click a row to open it. On either canvas, ← Stemmas goes back to the index, and a click on the name beside it renames the stemma.

Which layout

A rota suits a hierarchy you want to walk: a theme and its motifs, a faction and its cells, a decision and its branches. Sixteen children fit around each node, and there's no limit on depth. A tabula suits nearly everything else: a scratch board of notes, a table of columns, a lineage drawn as an org chart, a board for each act with a board for each of its scenes inside it. A stemma keeps the layout it was made with; there's no turning a rota into a tabula or back.

Linked to the rest of the world

A rota's linked node and a tabula's linked card point at a record of the world: an entry, an Astronomical Body, a map, a Glossopoeia or Fabula record, or another stemma. They take the record's icon and color and show its current name, so a rename follows on its own. Delete the record and the node or card becomes an orphan, faded and still showing the name it had when you linked it, until you re-link it or convert it to a freeform node or a note. Stemmas are records too. An Entry Reference field can point at one (a story's Brainstorm field in Fabula does), a linked node in another stemma can, and Concordance draws each stemma as a node with a links edge to every record its nodes or cards point at. Deleting a stemma counts the references to it first, then clears them.

Undo

Both canvases have Undo and Redo on their top bar, and Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Y (Ctrl+Shift+Z redoes too). Every add, delete, move, conversion, and edit is a step, and a drag or a run of typing in one field is one step of its own. The history holds fifty steps and lasts while the canvas is open; leave the stemma and it's gone. It's Stemma's own, as the map editor's is: the rest of Cartulary still has no undo.

Stemma · The rota

A radial tree you walk into

The canvas

Open a rota and you get a single focal node at the center, ringed by sixteen fixed slots arranged clockwise from twelve o'clock. Each slot holds one child or stays empty. Click a child to drill in: that child becomes the new center, with its own ring of children around it. A breadcrumb across the top tracks the path you've come down; click any segment to jump back up, or Back to root to return to the top. A child that has children shows a short line radiating outward, so you can tell at a glance which nodes open into something and which are leaves. There's no depth cap: a tree goes as deep as you take it.

To zoom, hold Ctrl (Cmd on a Mac) and scroll, from 50% to 250% in steps of 1.15, or press + and −, with 0 back to 100%. Plain scroll pans the view once you're zoomed in far enough to overflow the pane. That's the reverse of Linea, where plain scroll zooms.

Two kinds of node

Every node is one of two kinds. A freeform node is yours to label, color, and give an icon, for ideas with no record behind them: "Act II climax", a morality theme, the missing letter the whole plot turns on. A linked node points at a record instead, an entry or any other record of the world, and takes its look from it, the record's icon and color, with a small chain badge ⛓ marking it as linked. Its label is the record's current name, so rename the record and the node follows. If the record is deleted, the node becomes an orphan: desaturated, its label in italics, a broken-link icon in place of the record's, and still showing the name it had when you linked it, so you remember what it stood for.

Adding nodes

Empty slots show a faint +. Click one, or press N to take the first empty slot from twelve o'clock, for the smart input: type, and up to eight records whose names match appear below as you go, prefix matches first, a record that isn't an entry with its source beside its name. Pick one, by mouse or with the arrow keys and Enter, and you get a linked node; press Enter without picking and you get a freeform node with what you typed as its label. Esc backs out.

The inspector

Click the focal node, the one at the center, to open the inspector on the right; a click on a child drills in, so to inspect a child, drill into it first. For a freeform node you edit its label, color, and icon there. For a linked node you get a preview card and, where the record can be opened, an Open button that takes you to it. The footer converts a node between the two kinds, Convert to linked entry one way and Convert to freeform the other, which keeps the name the node was linked under as its label. Re-link to different entry points a linked or orphaned node at another record. Delete node removes the node and its whole subtree, after a confirmation that counts what goes with it. The root is the one node you can't delete; to clear a whole rota, delete it from the index.

Reordering

Drag a child around the ring and it follows the pointer from slot to slot. Let go on an empty slot and it moves there; let go on a sibling's slot and the two swap, the sibling drawn in the child's old slot while you drag, so you see the trade before you make it. Esc mid-drag cancels, and a press that never moves is still a click that drills in. From the keyboard, Alt+Left and Alt+Right move the focused child to the next free slot, counterclockwise or clockwise.

Keys

  • Left, Right

    Walk the focus around the children, clockwise and back, wrapping round. With nothing focused, Right takes the first child clockwise from twelve o'clock and Left the last.

  • Enter, Backspace, Home

    Drill into the focused child; go up to the parent; go to the root.

  • Delete

    Removes the focused child, with the inspector's confirmation.

  • N

    Opens the smart input on the first empty slot.

  • Alt+Left, Alt+Right

    Move the focused child to the next free slot either way round.

  • Esc

    Closes the inspector, then clears the focus.

  • +, −, 0, Ctrl+scroll

    Zoom in, zoom out, back to 100%. Ctrl+scroll zooms too.

  • Ctrl+Z, Ctrl+Y, Ctrl+Shift+Z

    Undo and redo.

The keys rest while you're typing in a field, while the smart input is open, and during a drag. In a locked world the rota opens read-only: a press on a child still drills in and nothing moves; the walk, Enter, Backspace, Home, Esc, and the zoom still work; Delete, N, and Alt with the arrows do nothing.

Stemma · The tabula

A board for everything that isn't a tree

A tabula is a canvas without edges, in the spirit of a pinboard. Cards sit where you drop them, snapped to a 10-pixel grid, and keep their order front to back, a dragged card landing on top. Columns gather cards into stacks, lanes, and rows; a note, a linked card, or an image can carry columns underneath it; a board card holds a whole board inside it; and lines run between cards and stay stuck to them as they move.

The rail

The rail down the left side adds five kinds of card. A Note has a title and a Markdown body, where [[wiki-links]] resolve. A Link is a card for an entry or any other record of the world, the rota's linked node in card form, and orphans the same way. An Image is a picture from a file, or one dropped straight onto the canvas, stored the way a map's image is: in your images folder when one is connected, otherwise inside the save file, with a confirmation for anything over 1 MB. A Column is a stack that cards drop into. A Board is a board inside this one.

Click a rail button to add one at the center of the view, or drag it out onto the canvas to put it where you let go; a double-click on empty canvas makes a note. A new note, column, or board opens its title for typing straight away (Enter moves from a note's title to its body, Esc closes the fields), and a new Link opens the smart input right where you put it. Below the five card kinds sit the three line tools, covered under Lines below.

Moving around

Ctrl+scroll (Cmd+scroll on a Mac) zooms about the pointer, from 25% to 200% in steps of 1.15, and a pinch does the same. Plain scroll pans, and Shift+scroll pans sideways. Hold Space and drag, or drag with the middle button, to pan from anywhere; in a locked world, any drag on empty canvas pans. The bar in the canvas's corner has −, the zoom, +, and Fit, and the keys + and − zoom while 0 fits. Each board remembers where you left it, in this browser beside your theme and font size rather than in the world file; the first time you open one, it fits its cards to the view.

Cards

Click a card to select it, and the inspector opens on the right: a note's title, body, and color; a linked card's preview with Open, Re-link to a different record, and Convert to note; an image's caption and Replace image…; a column's title, color, and layout; a board's title, color, and icon, with Open board →; and on every card, Duplicate and Delete. Double-click a note or a column to edit it right on the card.

Every card has a grip, the ⋮⋮ strip in its left margin (a column's is its header), and the grip is the only part that drags: a press anywhere else selects, a double-click edits or opens, and nothing moves unless you take the grip. That's what lets you click into a note to follow a link without nudging it. The handle on a placed card's right edge sets its width, from 160 to 800 pixels, an image down to 80, and a column, or a card with stacks under it, up to 2400.

Selecting several

Shift+click adds a card to the selection or takes it back out, and a drag across empty canvas draws a marquee that selects every placed card it fully encloses, Shift adding them to what's already selected. With several selected, the panel on the right counts them. Drag any of their grips and the placed ones move together: onto the board by the same distance, into a column in their top-to-bottom order, or into a sub-board as they sit. The arrow keys nudge them by the grid. Ctrl+C copies, Ctrl+V pastes around the middle of the view (into another board or another tabula too), Ctrl+D duplicates beside the originals, Ctrl+A selects everything on the board, and Delete deletes.

Columns, lanes, and rows

Drag a card over a column and a line shows where it will land; let go and it joins the stack there. Drag it back out onto the canvas to free it. A column's Cards per row, 1 to 6, splits it into lanes side by side, each a stack with a title, so three short lanes read as a table with its headers; the cards keep their places when the count changes, and taking the count back down merges the lanes in order. A column dropped onto a column becomes a lane of it, and a lane can hold lanes in turn, as deep as you like. Drag the divider between two lanes to set the width of the one before it; the last lane takes whatever's left, and no lane goes under 80 pixels.

Lay out switches a column without lanes between Stack, its cards one under another, and Row, its cards side by side, each as wide as what it holds, the column growing to fit. Delete a column that holds cards and Cartulary asks first: Move out puts its cards on the canvas where they stood, and Delete everything takes them with it.

Stacks under a card

A note, a linked card, or an image can carry columns, drawn underneath it: Add a stack in its inspector, or drop a column onto the card. Stacks under it, 1 to 6, sets how many sit side by side, each a column with a title and cards, and the card's inspector counts what it holds. This is how a lineage draws as an org chart: a master as a note, the apprentices a row in its stack, each apprentice's own apprentices a row under them, the whole thing widening as it fills. Delete a card with stacks and the same question comes up; Move out puts the stacks on the canvas whole, where they stood.

Boards inside boards

A board card is a whole board inside this one, its cards and lines kept on it, and its face counts everything below it. Double-click it to open it (the inspector's Open board → does the same); the breadcrumb along the top walks you back out, and Back goes up one level. Drag a card onto a board card and it goes inside: a line marks the edge nearest the pointer, and the card lands beside the board's content on that side.

Lines

Below the card kinds, the rail has three line tools. Line places points click by click; a double-click, Enter, or a right-click finishes the line, and Esc throws it away. Freehand draws in one drag, simplified on release, with both ends free. Put a line's end down on a card and it sticks there, following the card wherever it goes; put it down off a card and it stays where you put it. Select picks a line to move it, drag its points (an end dropped on a card sticks, dragged off one it comes free), nudge it with the arrows, or delete it. The panel on the right holds the map palette's eight stroke colors, the width, Smooth, and Arrowheads (none, at the end, or at both ends), editing the selected line or setting the next one's defaults. Esc leaves the line tools for the cards.

Keys

  • Esc

    Throws away a line in progress, deselects a line, and leaves a line tool; otherwise closes the smart input, ends an edit, then deselects.

  • Delete, Backspace

    Delete the selection, or the selected line.

  • Arrows

    Nudge the selection, or the selected line, by the grid.

  • Ctrl+C, Ctrl+V, Ctrl+D, Ctrl+A

    Copy, paste, duplicate, select all.

  • +, −, 0, Space

    Zoom in, zoom out, fit. Space held turns a drag into a pan.

  • Ctrl+Z, Ctrl+Y, Ctrl+Shift+Z

    Undo and redo, fifty steps while the canvas is open.

In a locked world the tabula opens read-only: the rail and Undo and Redo are gone, the inspector's fields are disabled, any drag on empty canvas pans, and the view's keys still work.

Stemma · Import and export

A stemma in a file

JSON, both ways

Export, on a stemma's row in the index or on its canvas's top bar, downloads it as <slug>.stemma.json. A rota goes out in version 1 of the file's envelope and a tabula in version 2, so a Cartulary from before boards refuses a tabula's file outright rather than misreading it, and still imports a rota. Import on the index reads either. On the way in, every id is regenerated so nothing can collide with what you already have; a name already in use opens the Skip / Replace / Rename choice you've met elsewhere; and a linked node or card whose record doesn't exist in this world arrives as an orphan, with a count in the import flash. Matching across worlds goes by record id alone, with no fuzzy name fallback, so after a move between worlds the links usually want re-linking by hand. The file's shape is on the JSON Structure page.

Markdown, out only

Markdown, beside Export in both places, downloads <slug>.stemma.md for reading or pasting elsewhere: the name as a heading, the description, then the content. A rota is a nested list in slot order from twelve o'clock, the root first: a freeform node is its label, a linked node a [[wiki-link]] to its record's name (pasted into a longform field, it links when the record is an entry or a body), an orphan its old name marked unlinked. A tabula is its free cards in reading order, top to bottom and then left to right, as a list; each column is a heading with its cards listed under it, lanes headings a level down, stacks and rows nested lists under their card, a sub-board a section of its own, and an empty column _(empty)_. A note brings its body, an image its file name and caption. Lines don't export. The Markdown can't be imported back; the JSON is the copy that returns. Everything else that travels is on the Import & Export page.

Concordance

The whole web, in one view

Concordance is the relationship graph of your entire world. Every entry is a node, every cross-reference between entries is an edge, and the graph arranges itself by how things connect, then hands the arrangement over to you. Where Linea and Stemma each show one shape, a bloodline, a single mind-map, Concordance shows everything at once: who points at whom, across every template you've got.

What becomes an edge

Anything that points from one entry to another turns into a line. The sources, all of them:

  • Entry-ref fields

    A plain reference draws an arrow from the entry holding the field to the one it points at. Family-role refs draw with meaning: a Parent ref points parent to child, matching the family tree, and a Spouse ref draws a line with an arrowhead on both ends.

  • Wiki-links

    Every [[Name]] in any text field becomes an edge from the entry you wrote it in to the entry it resolves to.

  • Map pins

    A pin on a map that links to an entry connects the map to whatever the pin points at, marked with a 📍 on the edge.

  • Stemma links

    A stemma's linked nodes, in a rota, and its linked cards, on a tabula, connect the stemma to every record they point at, one edge per record, labeled links.

  • References buried deeper

    Entry-refs inside a repeating group's rows, and entry-ref cells inside a grid, both feed the graph as well, so a link counts no matter how deep in an entry's structure it sits.

Every edge is directed: the arrowhead tells you which entry references which.

Reading the graph

Each node is one entry, a small circle in its template's color with the entry's name beside it. Where an edge has a meaningful label, the field that made the connection (Father, Members, Affiliations), the label rides along the line, rotated to stay upright and sitting on the upper side wherever the line angles. When two entries connect through several fields in the same direction, the labels join into one with a // between them, like "Members // Council Members". When the link is reciprocal, each side declaring the other, the two labels split apart toward their own ends so they don't pile up in the middle. Long labels truncate. Labels from Description and Notes fields are dropped on purpose: a wiki-link buried in a description is a real connection, but its field name is just noise.

Hover a node and a panel opens in the corner with a quick summary of that entry: its name and template (icon and color), any tags, a lifespan block from its first date-range field (birth date if available, then the full year span), spouse relationships with marriage dates, and a row for each other populated field that isn't prose or a family-role reference. Below the facts, the first populated text field renders as a plain-text preview, markdown stripped, capped at 240 characters. A connection count at the bottom tells you how many edges touch this node in the current view. While the pane is open, everything unrelated dims so you can read that one entry's connections out of a crowded field. Secret fields stay hidden unless secrets are unlocked. Click a node to open that entry; the edges themselves aren't clickable.

Laying it out, and moving around

The first time the graph draws, a force simulation finds positions, related entries pulling together into clusters. But those positions aren't the point; they're a starting offer. Drag any node where you want it and it stays there. That drag freedom is the whole feature: lay your stars into an actual star map, your factions into a power diagram, your planes into a cosmology, any arrangement where the position of a thing carries meaning the force algorithm could never know. The layouts you build become part of the world file, surviving reload, export and re-import, and a move to another device.

New entries you haven't touched place themselves with a quick force pass around the pinned majority when they first appear, then their settled spots save too, so you're never made to lay out every new arrival by hand. Positions for deleted nodes get cleaned out when the world loads, so a delete leaves no ghosts in the saved layout.

To move around the canvas, scroll to zoom, from a fifth of normal size up to four times, and drag empty space to pan. Zoom buttons sit in the corner, with a reset that snaps the view back. Your zoom and position hold while you work, and a filter toggle doesn't throw them away.

The Templates filter

The side panel lists every template with a checkbox, and because each row carries that template's color, the panel doubles as the graph's legend, telling you what each color means. Each row also shows a count of how many nodes of that type are currently in the graph. Untick a template and its entries vanish from the graph along with every edge that touched them; tick it back and they return. All and None at the top flip everything at once. Nothing is ticked when Concordance opens, so the graph starts empty and a large world doesn't render as one tangle. Toggling the filter never disturbs the layout you've built: hidden nodes simply stop drawing, their saved positions waiting for them. A node and connection count runs along the bottom edge of the canvas so you can see the current totals at a glance.

The list includes more than your regular templates. Astronomical Bodies defined in Ephemeris appear as their own toggleable type, as do Glossopoeia entities -- ConLangs, Lexemes, Phonemes, Grammatical Values, Affixes, Word-Building Processes, Inflection Classes, and Writing Systems -- and, since v1.6, Maps. Since v1.7 stemmas have a row of their own, and Fabula's records sit behind one Fabula row that toggles all eleven kinds at once: a corpus connects to its stories, a story to its threads and themes, a cast record to its character, a scene to its POV, participants, setting, threads, and motifs, and so on. Any of these that reference entries (or each other) produce edges just like a field on a regular template would. Toggle them off to reduce visual noise when you're not focusing on those parts of the world.

Focus mode

A whole-world graph can be a lot to look at. Focus mode narrows it to one neighborhood. Type a name into the Focus box at the top of the side panel, which autocompletes from your world and suggests only entries whose templates are currently visible, and once it matches an entry exactly the graph collapses to that entry and what surrounds it. The Hops input beside it sets how wide "surrounds" reaches: one hop is direct connections only, two is friends of friends, and so on up to fifteen, though in a well-connected world anything past three or four tends to pull in nearly everything. Hops count links as undirected, so a connection counts the same whichever way its arrow points. Click clear to return to the full graph. If the entry you focused is hidden by the Templates filter, the graph quietly falls back to showing everything rather than leaving you staring at a blank canvas.

When it gets big

The force pass that places everything on first render is where a large, densely linked world starts to feel slow, a few thousand entries with plenty of cross-references being the rough point where you notice it. The cost is paid once, though: with positions saved, later loads restore the arrangement without re-running the simulation. The Templates filter and Focus mode help here as well, since a narrower graph settles faster when new entries arrive.

Kalendarium

Your world's calendars, all in one place

Kalendarium holds every calendar in the world: the primary reckoning that everything else anchors to, and any number of additional calendars running alongside it. A world can have one calendar or a dozen, each with its own months, eras, week structure, seasons, and moons. You edit them here, compare them side by side, and navigate the year as a grid. Dates you enter anywhere in Cartulary draw from whatever you build in this view.

The primary calendar

Every world has exactly one primary calendar. It's the system that every date in the world stores against internally: when you write a date on a character's birth, an event, a map pin, it's always in primary's terms underneath. The primary also sets the reference frame that additional calendars anchor to. Rename its months freely; existing dates redisplay under the new names without moving. The default world ships with Common Year as the primary: the twelve Gregorian months in a 365-day year with no leap day, since Kalendarium has no leap rules. You can strip it out and rebuild from scratch the moment you open the calendar editor.

Additional calendars

Click + New Calendar in the Kalendarium header to add a parallel calendar. Its structure is entirely independent: different months, different eras, a different week cycle, its own seasons and moons. Its only tie to the primary is one anchor date, set in the calendar's own editor, that says "Year 1, Month 1, Day 1 of this calendar falls on this date in the primary." Everything else derives from that single alignment. All the absolute-day arithmetic runs under the hood; you just see dates in whatever calendar you're working with.

To change the anchor, open the additional calendar's editor and click Re-pin Anchor. A date picker opens in primary's frame; click Save Anchor and every date in that secondary calendar shifts to match. The underlying dates don't move, only the displayed year-month-day changes. The primary's anchor is always absolute day zero by definition and has no Re-pin button.

To remove an additional calendar, click its Delete button on the index row. If any entries are bound to that calendar, the tool opens a conversion step first: pick a target calendar, and all those entries re-bind before the calendar is removed. The primary calendar has no Delete button.

What's inside the calendar editor

Each calendar's editor has these sections, in this order.

Months. Add months with + Add Month, drag the handle on any row to reorder, set the name and day count per month, and type a short form (up to three characters) in the Abbr field. The short form is what the abbreviated date formats use. Day counts can be any integer; nothing requires the year to total 365.

Reordering months is safe: dates track by month name, not by position, so moving a month from slot 1 to slot 6 takes all the dates in it along. Deletion is different. When you delete a month, dates that lived in it roll forward by name: the tool looks for the next month in the old order that still exists and can hold the original day number. If no later month qualifies, those dates shift to the first month of the following year. The editor tells you how many dates are affected before you confirm.

Date format. A dropdown with five display presets that control how dates render across the app for this calendar: 3 January, 2026 (default), 03 JAN 2026, 3 JAN 2026, January 3, 2026, and 2026 JAN 03. The three abbreviated forms use each month's short form from the Abbr field above.

Eras. Optional. Each era has a full name, a short name, and a start year. A year falls into the era with the highest start year that's still at or before it. Define "Before Binding" starting at a very low number and "After Binding" starting at year 0, and year 5 displays as "5 AB" while year -3 displays as "-3 BB." Eras are display labels only; they don't change the stored values or the math.

Week. Set how many days are in a week, and optionally name each day. Day names are entirely optional; leave them blank and the grid renders unlabeled columns. The week cycle is continuous across month boundaries, so Day 1 of any month falls in whichever column the math puts it, not necessarily the first one. Leading blank cells fill the gap before Day 1 in the grid view.

Celestials. Moons are defined once in Ephemeris, under the Astronomical Body workspace, and referenced here by id. Toggle which of the world's moons this calendar shows; the grid renders a phase glyph on each day cell for each selected moon, up to five per calendar (more would crowd the cells). Phase resolves in order: geometric first, from the moon's actual orbital elements if it has a full orbit chain; synodic cycle as a fallback if the moon has a cycle length but no orbit; no glyph if neither applies. A moon's color comes from its Ephemeris kind and can't be overridden per-calendar. If you don't have any moons yet, + New moon quick-creates one with placeholder values and adds it to the calendar immediately; flesh out its orbit in Ephemeris later.

Seasons. Bands by day-of-year that tint the calendar grid. Each season needs a name, a start day, an end day, and a color. Day-of-year numbering starts at 1. To make a season wrap through the year-end, like a winter running from day 335 through day 59 of the next year, set the end day lower than the start day; the editor labels it "wraps year-end" as confirmation. Seasons can overlap or leave gaps. A seasons legend strip appears in the grid controls once at least one is defined.

Notes. A free-form text field for anything about the calendar system that doesn't fit elsewhere.

The calendar grid

Each row in Kalendarium has a Show grid button. Click it to expand an inline year-at-a-glance view below the row, with every month laid out side by side and every day as a clickable cell. The button collapses it again.

Above the month grids, the controls have previous and next year buttons, a Jump to... input, and a Go button. If the world has a current moment set, an ↺ Current button also appears; click it to jump to the year containing the current moment, projected into this calendar's frame. The year you were viewing persists per-calendar, so reopening a grid drops you back where you left off.

Each day cell shows its day number. Cells with dated entries on them render with a gold accent border and a bold number. Click a cell to open a popover listing every entry dated on that day, each shown with its name and the field the date came from. Season tints shade each cell's background in its season color, and the seasons legend strip above the months shows which color means what. Moon phase glyphs sit below the day number on each cell; hovering shows a tooltip with each selected moon's phase name for that day.

Ctrl+scroll (Cmd+scroll on a Mac) over the grid zooms it, from half size down to two and a half times up. The zoom is ephemeral; closing and reopening the grid resets to 1x.

Current moment

A small panel at the top of the Kalendarium index lets you set the world's current moment: the story's "now." When set, character entries whose template has a Lifespan field display an auto-computed age suffix from current moment minus birth date. Deceased characters compute age from their death date instead, and show it regardless of whether a current moment is set. Click Set to pick a date in primary's frame, Edit to change it, and Clear to remove it. The Compute and Observe workspaces in Ephemeris also read the current moment to know where to put the sky.

Comparison strip and the reference calendar

When the world has additional calendars, each row in Kalendarium shows a strip: "Reference moment: [date in this calendar's terms]." The moment driving every strip comes from whichever calendar is currently pinned. The pin auto-moves to any calendar you edit, and you can set it explicitly with the Pin button on any row (it shows 📌 Pinned when active). Pin the primary to anchor comparisons against the world's spine; pin an additional calendar to see every other calendar expressed relative to that one instead.

Per-entry calendar binding

When the world has additional calendars, entry editors gain a Dates reckon in... selector. Pick a calendar there and every Calendar Date and Calendar Date Range field on that entry shows its values in the chosen calendar's terms. The underlying absolute day stays stable; only the displayed year-month-day changes. Leave it on the primary if you don't need per-entry reckoning.

Import and export

The Kalendarium header has Import and per-row Export buttons. Calendars travel as .calendar.json files, useful for moving a calendar between worlds or sharing one as a setting reference. If the imported name collides with an existing calendar, the tool asks before overwriting. Cartographia

Maps with meaning

Cartographia is the bound collection of maps. A map is a canvas: an image you bring, a blank sheet, or both, with a drawing on it (shapes, labels, stamps, and painted layers, in a layer stack) and clickable, draggable pins over it. Each pin can link to any other entry in the world, so maps are cross-referenced, time-aware, and part of the same graph as everything else: a pin that links to a city shows up in Concordance as an edge from the map to the city, and filters itself off the map when the story hasn't reached it yet. This page covers maps, pins, time mode, and the downloads; The editor covers drawing and painting, and Stamps and brushes the packs and the brush imports.

The index

Click Cartographia in the sidebar and you land on the map index. A Stamps panel sits at the top (a world has far fewer stamp packs than maps, so it goes above the list; it's described under Stamps and brushes), then every map in the world, sorted by most recently updated. Each row shows the map glyph ▣, the name, the pin count, "no image" when the map is a blank canvas, the last updated date, a preview of the Description, and any tags. Click a row to open the map. Delete from the row's Delete button, or from the map's own toolbar; the map, its drawing, and all its pins are removed, and entries the pins linked to are unaffected. When entries reference the map, the confirmation says how many, and those references are cleared.

Maps are their own records, not template entries: there is no map template and no map kind in the Template Builder. Every map carries the same three fields, Description, Scope / Region, and Notes. A world saved by v1.5 with map-kind templates is migrated on load: each map entry becomes a map record, the map-kind templates are dropped, and any extra fields a custom map template had are kept in the record but no longer shown, with a line in Notes naming them.

Creating a map

Click + New Map at the top of the index. The editor opens with the name and tags at the top, the canvas, then the three fields; Save needs a name, Cancel discards (and, in folder mode, puts back any layer files the session wrote). Open a saved map and click Edit to come back to the editor.

Set Image in the canvas toolbar brings an image in: with an image folder connected, the file stays on disk and only its name is saved; without one, the image embeds as base64 in the save, with a warning for files over 1 MB. An image map's canvas is the image's own size. Change Image swaps the image and keeps the drawing and the pins; Remove Image clears it and leaves them on the canvas.

Without an image the canvas is a blank vellum sheet, and a Canvas picker appears in the strip: Landscape 1600 × 1000, Portrait 1000 × 1600, Square 1200 × 1200, or Custom…, which opens width and height fields (whole units, 100 to 10000 a side; anything outside is clamped) that you apply with Set or Enter. Changing the size of a map that already has shapes, paint, or pins asks first, because nothing is transformed: shapes keep their coordinates and anything past the new edges is cut off, a painted layer is clipped, and pins keep their relative positions and shift against the drawing. Undo restores it. Sizes are drawing units, not screen pixels; the canvas is drawn at whatever width the page gives it.

Pins

Pin is the editor's default tool. Click anywhere on the canvas to drop a pin, with a mouse, a pen, or a finger. Clicking a pin selects it and opens the pin editor below the canvas. In the map's view (not editing), clicking a pin that's linked to an entry opens that entry; a drag pans the map instead. The editor has:

  • Label

    The text displayed on the pin. The first character of the label (uppercased) shows as the pin's marker initial. An empty label shows a bullet instead.

  • Linked Entry

    Any entry in the world, or any Astronomical Body defined in Ephemeris. The link is an edge in Concordance, from the map to the entry. If you later delete the linked entry, the pin shows "(missing entry)" instead of breaking silently.

  • Always visible

    A toggle that marks this pin permanent. Permanent pins show on the map regardless of the time scrubber. Use it for things that exist across the whole timeline: coastlines, mountain ranges, ancient ruins, anything the story predates.

  • Color

    Per-pin color override. Defaults to the world's accent gold. Click Reset to default to return to it.

  • Notes

    Freeform notes about this pin. Not the same as the linked entry's content; these are map-context notes.

Drag any pin to reposition it. Pins store their position as fractions of the canvas, so resizing the canvas on the page or zooming it doesn't drift them (changing a blank canvas's size does shift them against the drawing, as noted above). Click the canvas away from any pin to deselect. Click Done in the editor to close it or Delete Pin to remove the pin. Pins are not part of the drawing: they sit over it, they go through undo like everything else, and they are left out of the SVG and PNG downloads.

Looking at a map

In a map's view the canvas is a camera over the drawing. Drag it to pan. Ctrl+scroll (Cmd+scroll on a Mac) zooms about the pointer, a pinch does the same, and the bar in the canvas's corner has − and + buttons, the zoom, and Fit; the keys + and − zoom and 0 fits. Fit is the whole canvas and as far out as it goes; the range runs to 8x. Drag the bottom-right corner of the canvas to resize the box on the page, from 25% of the column up to 150% (past 100% it grows from the center, as far as the pane allows); the bar says "NN% wide" when it isn't 100%, and double-clicking the corner snaps back. Neither the zoom nor the size persists; both reset on navigation and on reload. The editor has the same controls and a Pan tool besides; see The editor.

A small stats bar below the canvas shows the pin count, how many are linked to entries, and in time mode, counts of permanent and hidden-by-filter pins.

Time mode

When at least one pinned entry has a Calendar Date Range field with data, a Time button appears above the canvas. Toggle it on to add the year scrubber, and the map starts filtering pins by whether the current year falls within each pin's linked entry's range. Three pin states in time mode:

  • Permanent pins

    Marked "Always visible" in the pin editor. Always show, regardless of the scrubber. When time mode is on, a small ◇ marks them so you can tell them apart from temporal pins.

  • Temporal pins

    Linked to an entry with a date range. Visible only when the scrubber year falls within that range. In time mode, pin labels show the entry's name as it was at the scrubber year, so a pin linked to "New York" shows "New Amsterdam" when the year is 1640 if the entry's Former Names says so.

  • Linked pins with no date data

    Hidden when time mode is on. The interpretation is "you haven't filled in dates yet." If a pin should be visible across all time, mark it permanent explicitly.

Unlinked pins (no linked entry at all) are always visible; there's nothing to filter against. Toggle show faded to render hidden pins dimmed rather than invisible, so you can see "there was something here" without cluttering the map.

The scrubber's range comes from the actual data: the minimum and maximum years across all pinned entries' date ranges. Move through it with the range slider, the numeric year input, or the − and + step buttons beside it. Era buttons jump to that era's start year. If the world has multiple calendars, a calendar selector lets you drive the scrubber in any calendar's reckoning.

A few rules worth knowing. Only the first Calendar Date Range field on a linked entry's template counts; if an entry has multiple range fields, the others are ignored. Point-date fields (Calendar Date, not Calendar Date Range) don't affect pin visibility at all. Open-ended ranges work as you'd expect: a range with only a start year is visible from that year onward; a range with only an end year is visible up to it.

Downloading a map

In a map's view (not the editor), a Download row above the canvas writes the saved map as a file named after the map.

  • SVG

    The map as a standalone file: the drawing, with the image and every painted layer inlined as data URLs when they can be read, the stamps and tips it uses resolved into the file (each once, however many times it is placed), hidden layers left out, the map font embedded from the file's own copy (so labels keep their face when the file is opened elsewhere), and no pins. The root carries the canvas size, so other viewers and editors open it at the right dimensions.

  • PNG

    The same drawing rasterized at twice the canvas size. A canvas large enough that twice its size would pass sixteen million pixels is scaled down to fit, so a huge map writes a smaller file rather than a blank one.

A note beside the buttons says what was written and at what size. When the map's image or a painted layer can't be read (a folder file with no image folder connected, or a file the folder no longer has), the file is written with the rest of the drawing and the note names what is missing. Pins are app data with their own renderer, so they are never in the file; the world's HTML export is where a map travels with its pins, drawn through the same renderer as the app, painted layers included.

Limitations

No pen pressure: a stroke is the width you set however hard you press. No colors beyond the palette of eight for shapes, labels, and paint (a stamp pack can carry its own colors, and a color tip paints as it is). No grid, no snapping beyond the rotation knob's Shift snap, no fog of war, no measurement or scale tools, no layer opacity or blend modes, and no placing a shape or a stroke from the keyboard. Cartulary's map support is reference-grade: a drawn, painted, or imported map with clickable, cross-referenced, time-aware pins. If you need a virtual tabletop experience, that's a different tool.

Cartographia · The editor

Drawing and painting on a map

The map editor opens from + New Map or Edit on a map. Above the canvas sit the image buttons and the hint, a fixed two-line space that says what the active tool does, or why it can't when the active layer is hidden, locked, or the wrong kind; then the strip: the tools, the canvas controls, and the style of the next shape or the selected one. Everything here works with a mouse, a pen, or a finger, and most of it from the keyboard too.

The strip

The strip is built from named groups laid out in rows. As shipped: row one, the thirteen tools; row two, the Canvas picker (image-less maps only), Undo and Redo, Layers with the Layer select naming the layer new shapes go to, and Arrange…; row three, the Fill and Stroke swatches, Width, Smooth, and the active tool's own controls: the point count with Done and Cancel while a polygon or line is open, tip, size, and opacity for the Brush and Eraser, the stamp picks, a label's text, size, and arc, the angle of a label or stamp, the selected shape's layer, and Delete shape. Groups that don't apply to the current tool or selection dim rather than disappear, so nothing moves under the pointer when the tool changes.

Arrange… opens a modal where every group can be moved earlier or later, a "new row" checkbox starts a row before any group but the first, and the tool buttons can be reordered. Each change lands in the strip behind the modal at once, Reset restores the shipped layout, and the layout is kept in this browser with the theme and the font size, not in the world file.

The tools

Each has a letter that selects it when no text field has focus, shown in its button's tooltip with the hint. In the shipped order:

  • Pin (N)

    The default. Click the map to add a pin, click a pin to edit it, drag a pin to move it. The drawing is inert under this tool.

  • Select (V)

    Click a shape to select it. Drag it to move it. Drag a point to reshape a polygon or line, or a corner to resize a rectangle, ellipse, or stamp (a stamp keeps its proportions; nothing goes under 8 units). Drag the knob above a label or a stamp to turn it; Shift snaps the angle to 15°. The arrow keys nudge the selection by one unit, ten with Shift. Delete or Backspace removes it, Esc clears it, and a right-click returns to Pin. A line or polygon with more than forty points moves as a whole, a filled region has no handles, and shapes on hidden or locked layers can't be selected.

  • Rect (R)

    Drag on the map to draw a rectangle. A drag under two units in either direction places nothing.

  • Ellipse (E)

    Drag on the map to draw an ellipse.

  • Polygon (P)

    Click to place the corners of a closed, filled area. Clicking the first corner again, a double-click, Enter, Done in the strip (from three points), or a right-click closes it; Esc or Cancel discards it. The overlay shows the shape so far, a rubber line to the cursor, and the closing line.

  • Line (L)

    Click to place the points of an open line. A double-click, Enter, Done (from two points), or a right-click finishes it; Esc or Cancel discards it.

  • Freehand (F)

    Drag to draw a line in one motion. On release the line is simplified to the points it needs (a long coastline lands at a few dozen), it keeps Smooth on, and the tool stays so the next drag starts at once. Esc while dragging discards it. A freehand line of forty points or fewer reshapes by its points in Select; a longer one moves as a whole.

  • Fill (G)

    Click inside an enclosed area to fill it with the Fill color. The drawing as it stands is traced, so lines, shapes, the image, and painted layers all bound the fill; small gaps in the outline close on their own, and the fill lands just under the lines around it, above the topmost filled shape under the click in the active layer. A click too close to a line is refused with a note, as is an area too narrow to fill. The result is a region shape: selectable, movable, restyled by the swatches (it lands with no stroke; give it one in Select), with Smooth off because a traced edge is already true.

  • Text (T)

    Click on the map to place a label. The strip's text field takes focus so you can type over "Label"; Enter or Esc leaves the field. Size, angle, and arc are in the strip. A label is Ink until the Fill swatches restyle it.

  • Stamp (S)

    Pick a symbol in the strip (the four built-ins are always there; More… opens a grid of every stamp in the world, grouped by pack, with a filter), then click to place it 120 units across centred on the click, or drag to place it with its long side at the drag's larger side. An svg symbol places square; a raster tip keeps its own proportions. A placed stamp is selected, so the angle controls and the handles apply to it at once.

  • Brush (B)

    Paints on the active raster layer, or makes one above the active vector layer. See Painting.

  • Eraser (X)

    Erases from the active raster layer. See Painting.

  • Pan (H)

    Drag to move the view. See The view.

In any drawing tool a right-click finishes the shape in progress and switches to Select, with that shape selected; in Select a right-click returns to Pin. Esc discards a shape or a stroke in progress before it clears a selection. The browser's own menu never opens on the canvas.

Style

The style groups set the style of the next shape, or restyle the selected one; with a shape selected, the change becomes the default too.

  • Fill and Stroke

    Swatches from a fixed palette of eight: Ink, Sepia, Parchment, Forest, Water, Stone, Blood, Chalk, plus None, each named for a screen reader. New shapes start with a stone fill and a sepia stroke. A line has no fill, a bucket fill has no stroke until you give it one, and the Brush paints in the Fill color (Ink when Fill is None).

  • Width

    A slider from 1 to 32 drawing units, with a number field that goes to 64; new shapes start at 4. A stamp has no width control; its line weight is the symbol's own and scales with it.

  • Smooth

    Rounds a rectangle's corners, and curves a polygon, line, or region through its points instead of joining them with straight segments. On by default.

  • Text, Size, Arc, Angle

    For a label: its words, one of three sizes (24, 36, 56 units, in the map's Cormorant Garamond), an Arc slider from −180° to 180° in steps of 5 (right arches the label up, left bends it down; Straight resets it), and an Angle field with −15 and +15 buttons. A stamp has the angle controls too.

  • Layer

    With more than one vector layer on the map, a select showing which layer the selected shape is on; pick another to move it there.

  • Delete shape

    Removes the selected shape. Delete and Backspace do the same.

Painting

A map's layers are vector (shapes) or raster (one bitmap). Brush paints on the active raster layer; with a vector layer active, the first stroke makes a raster layer named Paint just above it, which becomes the active layer. Eraser erases from the active raster layer and never makes one. A pen's eraser end erases with the Eraser's settings from any drawing tool, and the tool stays. There is no pressure: a stroke is the tip at the size you set. The tool's controls sit in the strip:

  • Tip

    The built-in Round and Soft round, plus any tintable tip the world's packs brought in (see Stamps and brushes); More… opens them all, with Import.

  • Size

    In drawing units: a slider from 4 to 256, a field to 1024. The ring on the canvas shows it.

  • Opacity

    5% to 100%. One stroke is flat at that strength however many times it crosses itself; strokes over each other add up. For the Eraser it is how much one stroke takes away.

  • Where the paint lives

    A painted layer's bitmap is a PNG at the canvas's size, capped at 4096 pixels on the long side; a loaded image keeps its own format and size under the same cap. With an image folder connected, each painted or loaded layer is a file in the folder named after the map and the layer (map-a3f2c1-layer-b7c9d2.png), rewritten by every stroke and by undo; without one, it embeds in the save file, with a warning past 1 MB when you load an image and a hard stop at 48 MB. Cancel on the map puts the folder back to what it held when the editor opened; Save leaves the files as they are.

  • Load image…

    In Layers, on a raster layer: fills it with a PNG, JPEG, or WebP scaled to cover the canvas width with its proportions kept; what runs past the bottom is clipped, and a JPEG stays a JPEG unless the cap resizes it. Clear empties the layer; undo brings the image back.

The shape tools don't draw on a raster layer and the paint tools don't paint on a vector one; the hint says which layer to pick. Painted layers travel with the map into the HTML export and the downloads.

Layers

Layers in the strip opens the layer list under it, top of the stack first (the last layer in the file draws on top). Each row has a pick button for the active layer (new shapes and strokes go there; the strip's Layer select does the same, marking raster, hidden, and locked layers), the name, editable in place, the shape count (a raster layer shows "image", "empty", or "file missing" instead), and:

  • Hide / Hidden

    Toggles the layer's visibility. A hidden layer is skipped on screen, in the HTML export, and in the downloads.

  • Lock / Locked

    A locked layer can't be drawn or painted on and nothing on it can be selected.

  • ↑ and ↓

    Move the layer up or down the stack.

  • ×

    Deletes the layer. It asks when the layer has shapes or an image, and undo brings it back. The last layer can't be deleted.

  • + Add layer, + Add raster layer

    Adds a vector or a raster layer on top of the stack and makes it active.

Every map starts with one vector layer named Drawing. If the active layer is hidden or locked, drawing is refused and the hint says which, rather than quietly drawing on another layer. Layers are a named, ordered, show-or-hide, lock-or-unlock list and nothing more: no opacity, no blend modes, no per-layer export.

Undo and redo

Undo and Redo in the strip, or Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Shift+Z (Ctrl+Y also redoes), step through the last fifty changes to the map: one step per gesture, so a drag of any length, a run of typing in one field, a run of arrow nudges, a rename, or one paint stroke undoes in one go. Pins are included, and so is a layer deletion. Painted bitmaps are kept for undo up to 64 MB across the history, the oldest steps dropped first when they pass it; in folder mode undo rewrites the layer's file. The history belongs to the editing session: it's gone once you Save or Cancel, and it covers the map only. The rest of Cartulary has no undo; text fields keep the browser's own.

The view

The canvas is a camera over the drawing. Ctrl+scroll (Cmd+scroll on a Mac) zooms about the pointer, a pinch does the same, the bar in the canvas's corner has − and + buttons, the zoom, and Fit, and the keys + and − zoom while 0 fits. The range is Fit (the whole canvas) to 8x, in steps of 1.25; nothing goes below Fit. To pan, use the Pan tool, hold Space and drag from any tool, drag with the middle button, or drag with two fingers; two fingers anywhere also pinch, and cancel whatever one finger was doing. The zoom and the keys wait while a stroke, a shape, or a drag is in progress.

The grip at the canvas's bottom-right corner sets the box's width on the page, 25% to 150% of the column (past 100% it grows from the center, as far as the pane allows; the bar says "NN% wide" when it isn't 100%); double-click it to snap back. The zoom lives inside the box, handles and pins keep their screen size at any zoom, and nothing here is stored: a map opens at Fit.

Keys and pointers

Letters select tools (above) when no field has focus. Otherwise:

  • Arrows

    Nudge the selected shape by a unit; Shift+arrows by ten.

  • Delete, Backspace, Esc, Enter

    Delete or Backspace removes the selected shape. Esc discards a stroke or shape in progress, then clears the selection. Enter finishes a polygon or line.

  • Ctrl+Z, Ctrl+Shift+Z, Ctrl+Y

    Undo and redo.

  • +, −, 0, Ctrl+scroll, Space, middle button

    Zoom in, zoom out, fit. Ctrl+scroll zooms about the pointer. Space held turns any drag into a pan; so does the middle button.

  • Right-click

    Finishes the shape in progress and goes to Select; in Select, goes to Pin. The browser's menu never opens on the canvas.

  • Pen

    Draws, paints, selects, and drags like a mouse, without pressure; its eraser end erases.

  • Touch

    One finger does what the tool does (in Pan, it pans); two fingers pinch and pan from any tool and cancel the one-finger gesture they land on; a long press mid-stroke is not a right-click. In a map's view (not the editor), a finger's up-and-down swipe scrolls the page and a sideways drag pans the map.

  • Screen readers

    Every swatch is named ("Fill: Water"), every tool button says its letter and what it does, and a live region names the tool and the selected shape ("Rectangle, 200 by 100"). The canvas itself is a drawing surface and can't be driven from the keyboard.

Cartographia · Stamps and brushes

Symbols to place, tips to paint with

A stamp is a symbol the map editor's Stamp tool places and sizes; a tintable stamp is also a tip the Brush paints with. Four symbols are built in (mountain, tree, settlement, compass rose) and two tips (Round, Soft round); everything else comes in through the Stamps panel at the top of the Cartographia index, or through Import pack or brushes inside the editor's stamp and tip pickers. Stamps are world data: they travel in the world's .json and are listed in JSON Structure.

The Stamps panel

The panel lists every imported stamp grouped by pack, with a preview (a tip's tooltip gives its kind and pixel size), and offers:

  • + Import pack or brushes

    Reads one file or several; the routes are below. An import with nothing to report lands at once; one where something was changed, dropped, or needs a name shows a report first, with Cancel. A brush or image file handed to World settings' Import .json instead is refused with a pointer back here.

  • Export built-ins as a pack

    Writes the four built-in symbols as a correct pack file. Edit it, rename it, and import it to make your own.

  • Built-ins as PNG zip

    The four symbols as PNGs in the Wonderdraft folder layout.

  • Export, PNG zip

    On a pack: Export writes it back out as a .cartulary-stamps.json with its own ids, so a re-import replaces its own stamps; PNG zip writes it as PNGs in the Wonderdraft layout (below).

  • Delete pack, and × on a stamp

    Removes a pack with all its stamps, or one stamp. When the stamps are placed on maps, the confirmation says how many placements will go missing.

What imports

  • A Cartulary pack

    A .json holding svg symbols, PNG tips, or both; the format is below.

  • PNG files

    As a pack named Imported stamps, which you can rename in the report. A PNG with any transparency uses its alpha as the shape; a fully opaque one is read as a mask with black as ink. Any colored pixel makes it a color stamp, placed as it is; without one it is a tintable tip, drawn in the map's Fill color.

  • A zip

    Every folder of PNGs inside becomes a pack named after the folder, and a Wonderdraft .wonderdraft_symbols file beside them supplies the names and whether each is tinted (custom_colors) or placed as is (normal). Every .abr, .brush, and .brushset inside is its own pack, and .gbr and .gih files make a pack per folder, so a brush set's download zip imports as it came.

  • A Photoshop .abr

    Every sampled tip, with its name and spacing where the file records them. Versions 1, 2, and 6 read whole; 7 and 10 read where their layout matches. A computed brush has no bitmap and is skipped with a note, and a file with no sampled tip is refused with what it found.

  • GIMP brushes

    A .gbr is one tip, a .gih is one tip per cell. A color brush imports as a color stamp.

  • Procreate .brush and .brushset

    Each brush's Shape.png is the tip, read by brightness (white is the shape), and Grain.png is ignored. The name comes from the brush's own archive, else its folder; a brushset is named from its plist.

Tips are scaled down to 1024 pixels on the long side (the report says when), capped at 4 MB each, and a pack holds at most 500 stamps. The report shows the pack's name (editable for brush and PNG imports), what will import and what it replaces, what changed, what was dropped and why; a zip of several packs steps through them with Skip.

Tips and symbols

An svg symbol takes the map's Fill and Stroke like any shape. A tintable tip (the pack file calls it alpha) is drawn in the Fill color, Ink when Fill is None, and is what the Brush paints with; a color stamp (color) is placed as it is and never paints. A raster stamp's placement box keeps the tip's proportions at 120 units on its long side; an svg symbol's is square. A used tip goes into a map's file once, however many times it is placed.

The pickers

More… in the strip opens every stamp in the world (or, for the Brush and Eraser, every tip), grouped by pack with the built-ins first. Type to filter by name or pack name, with a count; a click arms the stamp and swaps the selected stamp's symbol, which is also how a missing one is re-pointed; Escape clears the filter, then closes. Import pack or brushes at the bottom imports without leaving the editor.

A pack file

A pack looks like this:

{
  "cartulary_mapstamp_pack": 1,
  "id":     "forest",                   // optional; lower-case letters, digits, underscore, dash. Defaults to a slug of the name
  "name":   "Forest",
  "stamps": [
    {                                   // an svg symbol
      "id":      "pine",                // required; same characters as the pack id
      "name":    "Pine",
      "viewBox": "0 0 64 64",           // the box the body is drawn in
      "body":    "<path d=\"M32 4 L19 26 L45 26 Z\" stroke-width=\"3\"/><path d=\"M29 58 V63 H35 V58\" fill=\"none\"/>"
    },
    {                                   // a raster tip
      "id":      "moss",
      "name":    "Moss",
      "kind":    "alpha",               // alpha: tinted by the map's Fill, and a Brush tip; color: placed as it is
      "image":   "data:image/png;base64,...",   // a PNG, at most 1024 pixels on the long side and 4 MB
      "width":   256,                   // pixels
      "height":  256,
      "spacing": 25                     // the gap between dabs when it paints, as a percentage of the size
    },
    ...
  ]
}

The body is SVG inner markup. Parts with no fill or stroke of their own take the colors the map gives the stamp, so leave those off wherever the stamp should follow the palette, and put fill="none" on parts that must stay hollow. Line weight is the stamp's own and scales with the stamp. A pack holds at most 500 stamps, and a body at most 200 KB. A raster entry's image is decoded and re-encoded on import (any colored pixel makes it color whatever kind says) and only checked for its shape on load, never decoded there.

Only these elements survive import: g, path, circle, ellipse, rect, polygon, polyline, line. Only these attributes: d, points, cx, cy, r, rx, ry, x, y, x1, y1, x2, y2, width, height, fill, fill-rule, fill-opacity, stroke, stroke-width, stroke-linecap, stroke-linejoin, stroke-dasharray, stroke-opacity, stroke-miterlimit, opacity, transform; values are plain numbers, path data, transforms, and colors, never url(...). Everything else (scripts, event handlers, style, href, text, images, use) is removed, on import and again whenever the world loads, and the import shows what it removed before anything lands.

Stamp ids are namespaced by the pack (forest__pine; pack and stamp ids are at most forty characters each), so two packs can each have a tree and neither touches the built-in one; re-importing a pack replaces its own stamps in place, and every placed stamp keeps pointing at the right symbol. A placed stamp whose pack is gone shows as a dashed box with a question mark in the editor, and the strip says which stamp is missing; pick another symbol for it, delete it, or import the pack again. The view, the HTML export, and the downloads leave a missing stamp out.

Exports

Export on a pack writes it back out as a .cartulary-stamps.json with its own ids, so a re-import replaces its own stamps. PNG zip writes it as PNGs in the Wonderdraft folder layout (<pack>/sprites/symbols/<pack>/ with a .wonderdraft_symbols beside them), svg symbols rasterized at 512 pixels white on transparent, so the zip unpacks into Wonderdraft's assets folder and Inkarnate and Dungeondraft take the PNGs inside. The built-ins export both ways too.

Timelines

Two views of the same dates

Cartulary has two timeline views. Vertical is a chronological list: events stacked top to bottom, grouped by year and month, readable at a glance. Chronometric is a horizontal chart: proportional duration bars, parallel lanes per template, zoomable from centuries down to individual days. Both draw from the same data. Pick the one that fits what you’re trying to see.

What feeds both views

Both timelines read every Calendar Date and Calendar Date Range field on every entry whose template is currently toggled on. There’s nothing to configure separately: add a Calendar Date field to a template, enter a date on an entry, and it shows up on both views. Calendar Date Range fields produce span bars on Chronometric and two separate list entries on Vertical (one for the start, one for the end, each labeled with “· start” or “· end”). Dates inside Repeating Group subfields are picked up as well. A date nested inside a group row surfaces on the timeline just like a top-level date field.

Both views have a template filter in the controls: one checkbox per template, with all and none links. Unticking a template hides all of its entries’ dates from the view. The two views handle the filter differently. Vertical resets to all-on every time you reopen the view; it doesn’t remember which templates you unticked. Chronometric keeps its filter for the session, surviving navigation away and back, and resets only on reload.

When the world has multiple calendars, both views offer a calendar selector. Every event gets projected into whichever calendar you pick: an event stored in the Elven reckoning displays under the correct Imperial year when you switch the view calendar, with no data loss.

Both views resolve entry names as of the event’s year. A date on the New York entry that falls in 1640 shows “New Amsterdam” if that entry’s Former Names covers the period.

Secret fields are hidden from both timelines unless the secrets visibility toggle is on.

Reading the list

Each event row shows the day number (or a compact “Month Day” label at year zoom), the entry’s template icon in its template color, the entry’s name, and a small meta line below it with the template name and the field the date came from. Calendar Date Range endpoints appear as two separate rows, each labeled “· start” or “· end” so you can tell them apart. Click any event row to open that entry.

    Year headers, then month sub-headers, then each event with a “Day N” prefix. The most granular view.

  • month

    Year headers, then month sub-headers, but no per-event day prefix. Good when day-level precision is more noise than signal.

  • year

    Year headers only, events listed flat beneath each year with a compact “Mon D” date label. Fastest to scan across long stretches of history.

Controls

The top controls bar has the template filter (checkboxes with all / none), the calendar selector (when the world has additional calendars), the three zoom buttons, and a Ctrl+scroll hint. That’s it; Vertical is deliberately simple.

Timelines · Chronometric

The horizontal chart

Lanes

Each template that has at least one dated event gets its own horizontal lane, labeled on the left. Within a lane, events that would overlap stack into sub-rows so nothing gets buried. Lanes grow taller to fit their actual row count; sparse lanes stay compact. A Marriages lane appears automatically when any characters have spouse references with Married or Ended dates set: one bar per marriage, deduped across the two reciprocal declarations, labeled with both partners’ names.

Navigation

The + and − buttons in the controls zoom in steps. Reset returns the view to its auto-fit state: the full date range of all visible events, with a small margin.

The Range inputs (From year / To year) let you pin the chart’s bounds to specific years instead of auto-fitting. Leave them blank to return to auto-fit.

Era bands from the view calendar render as faint background tints across the full chart height, with the era’s short name at the top of each band. The year axis adapts its tick density automatically: year labels at wide zoom, month labels at medium zoom, day numbers when zoomed in far enough.

Hover an event to see its entry name and field in a tooltip. In normal mode, click any event to open that entry. The Marriages lane is read-only on the chart: click a marriage bar to open the alphabetically-first partner’s entry; edit the dates by opening either spouse’s entry directly.

Edit mode

Click Edit in the controls (hidden when the world is locked) to enter edit mode. Ephemeris

Celestial mechanics for worldbuilders

Ephemeris is where you build a world’s sky: define its stars, planets, moons, and other bodies, then compute what the sky looks like on any date and what alignments those positions produce. The goal is a calendar of meaningful sky events you can hang lore on: conjunctions, eclipses, moon phases, festivals pinned to a real orbital moment. It is a worldbuilding tool, not a physics simulator. Bodies move on fixed Keplerian orbits around their parents. Mutual gravity, orbital decay, and collisions are out of scope by design.

The body tree

A panel on the left side of Ephemeris shows all the celestial bodies in the world, organized as a tree. Bodies nest by their parent: a moon under its planet, planets under their star, top-level bodies at the root. The tree has two sections, Computable and Narrative. Computable bodies participate in orbital math, phase calculations, and alignment queries. Narrative bodies exist for worldbuilding organization: galaxies, wormholes, megastructures, and similar things where Keplerian orbits don’t make sense. You can define them and put them in the tree without supplying any numbers.

The search box at the top of the tree filters by name or Other Names. The + button creates a new body. The body count and, in multi-select workspaces (Compute and Forced Alignment), the count of currently-selected bodies appears at the bottom of the panel.

Body selection behavior depends on the active workspace. In Astronomical Body and Observe, clicking a body selects it for the detail pane on the right. In Compute and Forced Alignment, clicking toggles the body’s membership in the current query; ineligible bodies are dimmed and unclickable.

Moons and calendars

A body of kind Moon is the same moon a calendar tracks for phase glyphs. Define the moon once in Ephemeris and reference it from any calendar’s Celestials section, or quick-create a new moon from the calendar editor and flesh out its orbit here later. Phase resolution follows a priority order: full geometric phase from orbital elements if the chain is complete, synodic-cycle fallback if not, no phase display if neither is available.

The current moment

Compute and Observe read the world’s current moment, the same anchor set in Kalendarium and World Settings. Move it and the sky moves with it. Observe can also step away from the current moment to any absolute day without changing the world’s stored moment.

Computable kinds

The nine computable kinds are Star, Planet, Moon, Dwarf Planet, Asteroid, Comet, Rogue Planet, Black Hole, and Meteor Shower. A body with one of these kinds can have orbital elements set, participates in Compute alignment queries, appears in the Observe diagram, and can be promoted to an Event entry. The narrative kinds (Galaxy, Nebula, Star System, and the rest) do none of those things.

What saves where

Body definitions are world data: they persist in the JSON save file under celestialBodies. Ephemeris workspace state (which mode is open, Compute query parameters, Observe zoom and display settings) persists in the save file under ephemeris but carries no world data. Dropping the ephemeris key from a JSON file loses only remembered UI positions, not any body.

Ephemeris · Astronomical Body

Building your system

Astronomical Body is the authoring workspace. Select a body from the tree to view or edit it, or create a new one with the + button in the tree panel or the + New Body button in the main area. If the world has no bodies yet, a tutorial button offers to seed a working Sol / Terra / Luna chain so you can see the math in action before entering your own numbers.

Body editor sections

The body editor opens in view mode when you select a body. Click Edit to enter edit mode, where all fields become writable. Save commits; Cancel discards.

Tags. Comma-separated tags, same as entries.

Identity. Name at the top of the editor (always editable in edit mode). Then:

  • Kind

    The structured taxonomy. Computable kinds (orbital math) are grouped separately from narrative kinds (lore only). Switching from a computable kind to a narrative kind hides the Orbital Elements and Physical Parameters sections in the editor; switching back reveals them.

  • Parent Body

    What this body orbits. Top-level bodies (typically a root star) have no parent. The Orbital Elements block only appears when a Parent Body is set; a body with no parent has no orbit to define. Orbits are the body’s motion relative to its parent.

  • Other Names

    Aliases. Searched by the tree’s search box and resolved by wiki-links in narrative text fields.

  • Type (narrative)

    A free-form label for worldbuilding use. Separate from Kind, which is the structured taxonomy driving the math.

  • Status

    Select with Other option: Active, Dead, Dormant, Destroyed, Extinct, Mythical, Unknown, Other.

  • Discovered / Founded

    A calendar-date field, with the approximate flag available.

Orbital Elements (computable kinds with a parent only). The six classical Keplerian elements. Each label carries a hover tooltip in plain language for worldbuilders who haven’t studied orbital mechanics. A Use sample values button fills in a working circular orbit at a scale typical for the body’s kind, useful for getting something orbiting quickly before you pick real numbers.

  • Semi-major axis

    How big the orbit is, in AU (astronomical units). 1 AU is the Earth-Sun distance. Bigger number = wider orbit = longer period.

  • Eccentricity

    How elliptical the orbit is. 0 is a perfect circle; values close to 1 are elongated. Capped at 0.99; parabolic and hyperbolic trajectories are out of scope. A slider and a numeric field stay in sync.

  • Inclination

    How tilted the orbit is relative to the reference plane, in degrees.

  • Ascending node (Ω)

    Where the tilted orbit crosses the reference plane going upward, in degrees. Swivels the orbit’s orientation around the parent.

  • Argument of periapsis (ω)

    Where in the orbit the body comes closest to its parent, in degrees from the ascending node.

  • Mean anomaly at epoch

    Where in the orbit the body sits at absolute day 0. 0° = starting at periapsis; 180° = starting at apoapsis.

Below the six inputs, Cartulary shows derived values inline: periapsis and apoapsis distances, orbital period (requires parent mass), and a tidal-lock indicator when rotation period is set.

Synodic cycle (Moon kind only). Two fields used as a fallback when geometric phase isn’t available: Synodic cycle (days per full phase cycle) and Phase anchor day (the absolute day on which this moon was at New). An indicator shows whether orbital geometry is driving phase or whether these fallback values are active.

Physical Parameters (computable kinds). Mass in Earth masses (Earth = 1, Sun ≈ 332,946), Radius in Earth radii, and Axial tilt in degrees. Mass feeds the orbital period derivation for children; a planet with no mass set means its moons can’t compute a period, and Compute / Forced Alignment will flag the chain as broken. Radius and axial tilt are display-only.

Below the inputs, a Derived values collapsible block shows secondary derivations: escape velocity, Hill sphere radius, Schwarzschild radius (black holes only), habitable zone (stars only), and synodic recurrence periods against sibling bodies. The block is collapsed by default and only appears when at least one derivation is computable.

Habitable zone (Star kind only, inside Physical Parameters). Three models: Conservative (Kasting 1993), Optimistic (Kopparapu 2013), and Manual bounds. The first two derive the zone from stellar mass via main-sequence physics (L = M^3.5); a Luminosity override field lets you supply the star’s actual output in solar luminosities instead, for giants or pre-main-sequence stars. Manual bounds takes explicit inner and outer AU values.

Physical (narrative). Free-text fields for Atmosphere, Gravity, and Rotation Period (days). Rotation period feeds the tidal-lock status indicator in the Orbital Elements block.

References. Links to entries and other bodies: Visible From (Location entries or other bodies), Notable Locations (Location entries), Inhabited By (Race / Species entries), Sacred to (Deity / Entity entries). Children of the current body appear here too, with clickable chips and an + Add child body button to create a new body pre-parented to this one.

Narrative. Six longform markdown text fields: Physical Properties, Climate / Conditions, Mythological Significance, Description, History, Notes. Wiki-links to entry names and body names resolve in view mode.

Display. A color picker for this body’s rendering in the Observe diagram. The color resolves in priority order: per-body override (set here) ← kind-level override (set in Observe’s kind colors panel) ← kind default. Reset to default clears the per-body override.

Deleting a body

A Delete button appears in view mode. Deleting a body removes it from the world, re-parents its children to null (they become top-level), scrubs all calendar references to it, and clears any entry-ref fields pointing to its id. The operation confirms before acting.

Ephemeris · Compute

Finding alignments

Compute takes a set of bodies and a date range, then scans for days when those bodies align in the way you specified. Select bodies by clicking them in the tree: eligible bodies are sorted to the top of each level; ineligible ones are dimmed. A body is ineligible if its orbit chain is broken (missing parent mass, or no semi-major axis set).

View modes

Compute has three view modes, switched from a tab row at the top of the workspace:

  • Split

    Query panel above, results below. A drag handle between them lets you adjust the split ratio. This is the default after running a query.

  • Query

    Full panel dedicated to the query form.

  • Results

    Full panel dedicated to the results list.

Query form

Alignment type. Four options:

  • Phase alignment

    Finds days when the selected bodies are at the same orbital phase relative to their shared parent: a conjunction at periapsis, all bodies at the same point in their cycles. Requires all selected bodies to share an immediate parent.

  • Conjunction

    Finds days when the bodies appear at the same angular position as seen from the central star. Bodies can be at different levels of the parent chain.

  • Eclipse

    Exactly two bodies: finds days when the first eclipses the second (covers a fraction of the second’s disk). Tolerance is eclipse magnitude (0–1, fraction of the eclipsed disk covered). Requires radius data on both bodies.

  • Nodal alignment

    Finds days when all selected bodies are near their ascending or descending nodes simultaneously. Bodies must share an immediate parent. Inclination must be greater than 0 on each body.

Date range. Start and end in the world’s primary calendar. The range is required; there’s no whole-history scan.

Tolerance. How close bodies must be to count as aligned, in degrees (or eclipse magnitude for eclipse type). Leave blank to use the default for each type. Defaults: phase 18° (5% of a full cycle), conjunction 1°, eclipse 0.5 magnitude, nodal 1°.

Switching alignment type after picking bodies may auto-deselect some: bodies that don't meet the new type's constraints (wrong parent, missing radius, etc.) are removed automatically with an inline notification showing the count.

Click Run or press Ctrl+Enter (Cmd+Enter on Mac) to execute the query. At least 2 bodies must be selected.

Saved queries. A name field and Save current button bookmark the current query settings. Saved queries appear below the form; click to load, delete with the × button. Query bookmarks survive mode switches and reloads.

Results

After running, results appear sorted by date. Each row shows the date, name, type, source badge, and the bodies involved. Click a row to open the detail drawer on the right side of the results pane.

The detail drawer shows the date, the bodies involved with their parent context, and a metric block specific to the alignment type. For phase: each body's mean anomaly at event time plus the spread in degrees. For conjunction: root-relative positions in AU and the maximum pairwise angular separation. For eclipse: the observer body, eclipse magnitude, and percent coverage. For nodal: each body's distance to its nearest node and the maximum distance across all bodies. All values show the tolerance for comparison.

Results are session-only; they don’t persist in the save file. Re-run the query after loading a world to regenerate them. To turn a computed result into a world entry, use the Event workspace.

Ephemeris · Forced Alignment

Designing when it happens

Forced Alignment runs Compute backwards. Instead of “where are these bodies on this date,” you decide when the alignment occurs and the workspace tells you what orbital values make it happen, or applies those values to the bodies directly.

The workflow: select bodies in the tree (same multi-select as Compute; same eligibility rules), fill in the target date and alignment type, and the preview table updates live as you type. No separate Run button: consequences appear immediately.

Inputs

  • Alignment type

    Phase, conjunction, eclipse, or nodal; same four types as Compute.

  • Target date

    The date you want the alignment to occur. Choose which calendar to express it in from a selector next to the date picker.

  • Recurrence interval

    Optional. Enter a number of days to force the alignment to recur on that cadence; the workspace rewrites both the mean anomaly at epoch and the semi-major axis to lock in a commensurate orbital period. Leave blank for a one-shot alignment (mean anomaly at epoch only is rewritten).

  • Phase fraction

    For phase-type alignments, a 0–1 fraction specifying where in the orbit “aligned” means: 0 = periapsis, 0.5 = apoapsis, etc.

Preview

The preview table shows each selected body with its current orbital values and the values Cartulary would write to force the alignment. Two sections: Eligible bodies (will be edited) and Ineligible bodies (flagged with a reason). An ineligible body is one that can’t be given the required orbit, typically a body with a broken chain or missing parent mass.

Apply

Click Apply to write the orbital values shown in the preview to the selected bodies. Cartulary confirms first, listing which fields will change. For one-shot alignments, only meanAnomalyAtEpoch is rewritten. For recurring alignments, semiMajorAxis is also rewritten to lock the period. For eclipse alignments, ascendingNode may also be rewritten.

After applying, a summary banner appears showing which bodies were edited. Three actions are available from the banner:

  • Verify in Compute

    Switches to the Compute workspace with the same bodies, alignment type, and target date pre-filled, so you can confirm the alignment appears as Compute would detect it.

  • Revert

    Restores each affected body’s pre-apply values for the specific fields that were changed. Manual edits to other fields, or to bodies that weren’t part of the apply, are preserved. Confirms before acting.

  • Dismiss

    Clears the banner without reverting anything.

The banner persists across mode switches and reloads until you dismiss or revert it. Revert is disabled when the world is locked.

Ephemeris · Observe

The orbital diagram

Observe draws a top-down 2D diagram of a body system at a given moment in time. Select any body in the tree: Observe walks up to the root ancestor and renders the full system from that root outward. Bodies whose orbit chain is broken appear in a warning banner and are left out of the diagram rather than drawn at a meaningless position.

Navigation

Drag to pan the diagram. Ctrl+scroll (Cmd+scroll on a Mac) zooms. The date at the top of the diagram shows the currently-rendered moment.

Date controls

A row of step buttons surrounds the current date display: −1y, −1mo, −1w, −1d, [date], +1d, +1w, +1mo, +1y. Month and year steps use the average days per calendar month and year respectively. A Now button to the right returns to the world’s current moment; when the view is following the current moment, the date display appends “· following current moment” and the Now button is highlighted. If no current moment is set in World Settings, Now is disabled. A dismissible banner explains why and points you to where to set it.

Display toggles

A row of controls governs what renders: four checkboxes and a Size selector.

  • Orbital paths

    The orbital ellipses for each body around its parent. Moon orbits render as dashed lines to distinguish them from planet orbits.

  • Body labels

    Body names on the diagram.

  • Grid

    Concentric distance rings with adaptive spacing (10ⁿ AU intervals scaled to the system’s extent).

  • Size

    A selector with two options, Visual (flat) and Actual (log-scaled). Visual gives every body a flat dot radius that’s easy to read at small scales; Actual scales by physical radius in Earth radii (logarithmically) and needs radius data.

  • Trails

    Recent-path arcs for each body. Trail window defaults to 365 days; a numeric input next to the toggle adjusts it. The trail is capped at 95% of the body’s orbital period so fast bodies don’t re-trace their full orbit.

Colors

Bodies are colored by kind by default (each kind has a default color). Two layers of overrides are available:

  • Kind colors

    A collapsible panel in Observe lets you override the color for an entire kind: all planets, all moons, etc.

  • Per-body color

    The Display block at the bottom of the Astronomical Body editor lets you set a color for one specific body, overriding the kind color.

Resolution priority: per-body override → kind override → kind default.

Saved viewpoints

A Viewpoints panel (collapsible, at the bottom of the controls) saves and restores snapshots of the current date, zoom, pan position, selected body, and display toggles. Name a viewpoint and click Save; click a saved viewpoint name to restore it. Delete individual viewpoints with the × button. Viewpoints persist in the world save file.

Ephemeris · Event

The sky event index

All three feed the same sortable, filterable list.

Reading the list

The table has five columns: Date, Name, Type, Source (a badge: Festival, Historical, or Computed), and Bodies / refs. Date, Name, Type, and Source are sortable: click a header to sort, click again to reverse. Bodies / refs is display-only. Click any row to open the detail drawer on the right.

Glossopoeia

A workbench for constructed languages

Glossopoeia is where you build a world’s invented languages, from the sound inventory up through morphology, grammar, the dictionary, the writing system, and glossed example texts. Each language is edited through an Overview container plus eleven workspaces. Pick a language from the selector at the top and every workspace shows that language’s data. A world holds as many languages as you like, and a language can be marked a dialect or a descendant of another.

It’s a view, not a template

Unlike characters or locations, languages aren’t entries built from a template. Glossopoeia is its own view with its own data, stored under glossopoeia in the save file. Each language is a ConLang container record; everything else (words, sounds, rules, affixes, and so on) is a separate record carrying a langId pointer back to its parent language. That structure is what lets the fork and sound-change tools copy a whole language at once.

An earlier build had a single “Language / Glossary” template. Worlds made with it migrate automatically: language-overview, dialect, and naming-convention entries become ConLang containers, and words, phrases, idioms, and every other entry become Lexemes. The five built-in templates that referenced the old template (Character, Location, Culture / People, Document, Race) re-point at ConLang. Any word whose language can’t be determined lands in the Unassigned bucket, described under Lexicon.

The language selector

The header runs across the top of every workspace. The Language dropdown lists every ConLang in the world, plus an Unassigned (N) option when orphaned words exist. Selecting a language opens its Overview; selecting Unassigned jumps straight to the Lexicon so you can re-home those words. To the right of the dropdown sit the language-level actions.

  • + New Language

    Creates a fresh, empty ConLang and opens its Overview.

  • Delete

    Removes the language and every record that belongs to it across its child arrays, after a confirm. It also scrubs any reference a surviving language held to one of the deleted sounds, so nothing dangles.

  • Export

    Writes the language and all its Glossopoeia data to a single .conlang.json file.

  • Grammar .md / Grammar .html

    Export a readable reference grammar and dictionary, as Markdown or as a printable HTML page.

  • Fork

    Creates a descendant language. Covered below.

  • Run sound changes

    Appears only when the language has a Descended From link. Re-derives every word’s pronunciation from its parent word. Covered below.

  • Import

    Loads a .conlang.json file as a new, independent language. Cross-language links that can’t be resolved in the new world are dropped, and the count is reported.

The workspace rail

A 200-pixel rail on the left lists the workspaces, with two collapsible groups. The order is Overview, then Phonology (Inventory, Phonotactics), Morphology, Noun Number, Pronouns, Verbs, then Grammatica (Syntax, Complex Structures), Lexicon, Orthography, Corpus. Noun Number and Verbs are read-only digests that pull from Morphology; they own no data of their own.

Forking and sound change

Fork and Run sound changes are the diachronic engine. Fork copies a language’s lexicon and inventory only into a new descendant: the child gets fresh copies of every word and sound, its Descended From points at the parent, and each child word’s Descended From points at its parent etymon. Grammar, syntax, and orthography are left empty for you to build. Morphology annotations on the copied words (Inflection Class, Features, Built By) are dropped, since the child has no morphology yet. The child is phonetically identical to the parent until you add sound changes and run them.

You write those changes in Phonology: Phonotactics as Phonological Rules with the Diachronic field set to Diachronic. Run sound changes then re-derives every descended word’s pronunciation by applying those rules to its parent form. A preview modal shows each parent form mapped to its daughter form, with changed and unchanged counts, a list of any rules it couldn’t apply automatically (insertions with no input sound, or prose-only changes), and an optional checkbox to prune sounds the run leaves unused. Nothing is written until you confirm.

Two related fields are easy to confuse. Variety Of marks a dialect or register at the same time depth; it’s a name-pointer for the family tree only, with no live inheritance. Descended From marks evolution by sound change across time and is the link the engine reads. A forked language is self-contained after the fork: importing or editing it doesn’t reach back into the parent.

What registers in Concordance

Eight of the twenty-one data arrays register as node types, meaning their records appear in Concordance and are valid targets for entry-reference fields: ConLangs, Lexemes, Phonemes, Grammatical Values, Affixes, Word-Building Processes, Inflection Classes, and Writing Systems. A Lexeme’s etymology points at other Lexemes; an Affix’s Marks points at Grammatical Values; a Grapheme’s Belongs To points at a Writing System and its Spells at Phonemes. The remaining arrays are view-internal; nothing outside Glossopoeia references them.

What saves where

Everything you enter is world data and persists under glossopoeia in the save file: the ConLang containers in conlangs, and every other record in its own array (lexemes, phonemes, and so on). The ui key under glossopoeia remembers only which language and workspace were last open; dropping it loses no language data. Collapsible workspace notes live under workspaceNotes, keyed by language and workspace.

A language and its grammar can also leave the world entirely, as a .conlang.json or a readable grammar export; see Import & Export for how that sits beside everything else Cartulary can share.

Glossopoeia · Overview

The language container

Overview is the ConLang container editor: the typological facts and cultural context for the language as a whole. It uses the standard entry form, so the fields behave exactly like fields on any template.

Fields

  • Other Names

    Alternate names the language goes by.

  • Status

    Where the language stands in use or history: Active, Archaic, Dead, Endangered, Forgotten, Liturgical, Lost, Reconstructed, Revived, Sacred, Secret, Unknown, or a custom Other value.

  • Autonym / Autonym Meaning

    The language’s own name for itself, and what that name means.

  • Morphological Type

    How words are built: Isolating, Agglutinative, Fusional, Polysynthetic, Templatic / Nonconcatenative, or Mixed.

  • Alignment

    How the language marks who did what to whom: Nominative-Accusative, Ergative-Absolutive, Tripartite, Active-Stative, or Mixed.

  • Basic Word Order

    SOV, SVO, VSO, VOS, OVS, OSV, or Free.

  • Variety Of

    A pointer to a parent language this one is a dialect of. Name-only, for the family tree; no live inheritance.

  • Descended From

    The parent language this one evolved from by sound change. Set automatically by a fork; this is the link the sound-change engine reads.

  • Cultural Position

    The language’s standing within its culture: Common, Feared, Forbidden, Regulated, Restricted, Revered, Sacred, Suppressed, Tolerated, Unknown, or Other.

  • Cultural Reach

    How widely it’s spoken: Local, Regional, Continental, Global, Cosmic, Variable, Unknown, or Other.

  • Spoken By

    Entry-references to the Cultures, Races, and Factions that speak it.

  • Region

    Entry-references to the Locations where it’s spoken.

  • Overview

    Free-form notes on the language as a whole.

Spoken By and Region are entry-references into the rest of the world, so a language and the peoples who speak it stay linked in Concordance. The closed typological dropdowns (Morphological Type, Alignment, Word Order) carry no Other; the in-world Status and Cultural fields keep Other so anything missing is still recordable.

Glossopoeia · Phonology

Sounds and the rules that govern them

Phonology splits into two workspaces: Inventory (the sounds themselves) and Phonotactics (how they combine and change).

Inventory

One entry per sound. The entry name is the phonemic symbol itself, entered through the IPA helper. A Class field (Consonant, Vowel, Diphthong, Tone) drives which articulatory fields appear:

  • Consonant

    Airstream, Manner, Place, Voicing, Laminality, Secondary Articulation (multi-select), Release (only for plosives and affricates), and Syllabicity.

  • Vowel

    Height, Backness, Roundedness, Nasalization, Tongue Root, and Rhoticity.

  • Diphthong

    Nucleus and Offglide (each a reference to another Phoneme, the vowels it’s built from) and Diphthong Type.

  • Tone

    Register and Contour.

Phonation and Length are shared by consonants and vowels. Every sound also carries a Romanization, a Status (Attested, Marginal, Reserved, Excluded, or a custom Other value; Excluded reveals a Reason field), Examples, and Notes. The workspace has a collapsible Romanization / pronunciation-key note at the top and, when the language has sounds, a Remove unused sounds button that prunes sounds no word uses and no constraint or active rule needs (removing one also clears it from any rule that named it).

Phonotactics

Four tabs. Syllable Structure and Stress are one-per-language specifications; Constraints and Rules are lists.

  • Syllable Structure

    Template (the syllable shape, like (C)(C)V(C)), Onset Requirement, Max Onset, Max Coda, Nucleus Types (multi-select), and Notes.

  • Stress

    Type (Fixed, Weight-sensitive, Lexical, Morphological, None), with Default Position shown for fixed stress and Weight Criteria for weight-sensitive stress, plus Secondary Stress and Notes.

  • Constraints

    Each Phonotactic Constraint has Polarity (Permitted or Prohibited), Position, an Applies When choice for onsets and codas (anywhere at that position, or only in a cluster), a Pattern, the Phonemes it involves (references), and Notes.

  • Rules

    Each Phonological Rule has Rule Type, Input, Output (leave empty or use the null symbol to delete the sound), Environment in slot notation, Order, Application (Obligatory, Optional, Variable), the Diachronic flag, Affected Phonemes (references), and Notes.

Environment uses slot notation: _ is the sound’s position, # a word edge, V or C any vowel or consonant, and a letter a specific sound. Blank means everywhere. So _ # is word-final and V _ V is between vowels.

The Diachronic flag is central to how the language behaves. A Synchronic rule is a living sound process and shows up in the surface-form readout in the Lexicon. A Diachronic rule is a historical change a fork ran over the parent’s words to derive this language; it’s skipped by the readout so it never reads as a sound the language still makes, and it’s the rule type Run sound changes applies. Blank counts as synchronic.

Glossopoeia · Morphology

The grammar toolbox

Morphology is a four-tab toolbox. All four entity types register as Concordance nodes, and the Lexicon and the other workspaces reference them.

  • Grammatical Value

    A single grammatical value like accusative or plural. Fields: Category (case, gender / class, number, person, tense, aspect, mood, and so on, plus Other), Short Tag (the gloss abbreviation, like ACC or PL), Function, Word Class (multi-select), and Notes.

  • Affix

    The entry name is the affix form itself (IPA helper). Fields: Position (prefix, suffix, infix, circumfix), Job (grammar ending vs. word-builder), then either Marks (references to Grammatical Values, shown for inflectional affixes) or Adds Meaning (shown for derivational affixes), Attaches To (multi-select), and Notes.

  • Word-Building

    A Word-Building Process: Method (compounding, reduplication, conversion, stem change, root-and-pattern, blending, clipping, or Other), How It Works, Takes and Makes (multi-select word classes), an Example, and Notes.

  • Inflection Class

    An ending-group a word can follow. Fields: Applies To (multi-select), an Endings grid (label the rows and columns, fill the endings), an Example word, and Notes.

Noun Number and Verbs

These two workspaces are read-only digests. They own no data; each filters the Morphology toolbox down to what’s relevant and shows it on cards, with the inflection-class endings grids rendered inline. Every card has a link back to its source so you can edit it in Morphology.

  • Noun Number

    Pulls together the Grammatical Values in the number category, the Affixes that mark any of those number values, and the Inflection Classes that apply to nouns.

  • Verbs

    Pulls together the Grammatical Values whose Word Class includes verb, the Affixes that attach to verbs, and the Inflection Classes that apply to verbs.

Because they’re derived views, anything you change in Morphology shows up here automatically, and there’s nothing to keep in sync by hand.

Glossopoeia · Pronouns

Pronoun sets

One entry per set. A Pronoun Set has a Type (personal, possessive, reflexive, reciprocal, demonstrative, interrogative, relative, indefinite, or Other), a Distinguishes multi-select for the axes it tracks (person, number, gender / class, case, clusivity, formality, animacy), a freeform Forms grid where you label the rows and columns and fill in the pronouns, and Notes.

Nothing references a pronoun set, so it doesn’t register as a Concordance node. It’s documentation, laid out the way you want it in the grid.

Glossopoeia · Grammatica

Syntax and complex structures

Grammatica is two workspaces. Syntax covers basic clause structure; Complex Structures covers relative and linked clauses.

Syntax

Three tabs. Phrase Order and Sentence Marking are one-per-language specifications; Constructions is a list.

  • Phrase Order

    Where each modifier sits relative to the noun: Adjective Order, Numeral Order, Demonstrative Order, Article Placement, Possessor Order, and Relative Clause Order, plus Adposition Type (prepositions, postpositions, both, or none) and Notes.

  • Sentence Marking

    Marks Core Roles By (case endings, word order, verb agreement, particles), Verb Agrees With and its Agreement Features (shown when the verb agrees with something), Word Order Rigidity, Dropped Subject OK, Dummy Subject, and Notes.

  • Constructions

    Each Construction documents a sentence pattern (negation, questions, commands, predication, comparison, possession, existentials, topic / focus fronting, or Other): how it’s formed, the Marker, its Position, any Word Order Effect, an Example, and Notes.

Complex Structures

Two tabs. Relativization is one per language; Clause Linking is a list.

  • Relativization

    Strategy (multi-select: relative pronoun, invariant marker, gap, resumptive pronoun, participle, correlative), the Relativizer, Gap or Resumptive, Verb Form, the Roles You Can Relativize (multi-select), whether restrictive and non-restrictive clauses are marked differently, an Example, and Notes.

  • Clause Linking

    Each entry documents one kind of clause-joining (coordination, complement, adverbial, conditional, serial verbs, switch-reference, clause chaining, reported speech, or Other): the specific Relation / Use, How It’s Joined, the Marker, Clause Form, any Distinctions, an Example, and Notes.

Glossopoeia · Lexicon

The dictionary

Lexicon is the dictionary: a word list on the left, the selected word’s detail on the right. Words are Lexemes, each carrying a langId back to its language. The list shows each word with its pronunciation beneath, sorted alphabetically, with a filter box once the language has more than six words.

The word detail

Above the entry form, three computed cards appear for the selected word:

  • Syllables

    The word auto-syllabified by maximal onset, refined by sonority and Word-Piece seams. A manual override field lets you correct the split, and any phonotactic-check warnings (a cluster the syllable structure forbids, for instance) show here.

  • Surface form

    The phonetic result of running the language’s synchronic sound rules on the word, with a toggle to see which rules fired. It renders nothing if no rule changes the word.

  • Paradigm

    The inflection table generated from the word’s stem plus its inflection class endings. Auto cells show the stem plus ending; accent-colored cells are irregular overrides from the word’s Irregular Forms; italic cells are stem-change instructions you fill by hand. It renders nothing if the word has no inflection class.

An important design point on those italic cells: a stem-change paradigm cell shows the instruction, it doesn’t execute it. The tool tells you a change is called for; you supply the actual form.

Lexeme fields

Below the cards is the full word form: Pronunciation (IPA), Part of Speech, Unit (word / phrase / idiom), Status, a repeating Senses group (gloss, definition, a per-sense part of speech and register, an example), a name block (Name Type, Name Meaning, Name Elements) that appears only when the part of speech is “proper noun (name),” Inflection Class and Features references into Morphology, Irregular Forms, Origin, Derived From, Source Word, Cognates, Descended From, Built By, a Word Pieces group (Sounds, Role, and an Affix reference, one row per piece), and Notes.

Two etymology fields are worth distinguishing. Source Word is for a loanword source or an older form you’re tracking by hand. Descended From is set by a fork and is the field the sound-change engine reads; it usually points at one parent word, more only for a merger.

Generate words

A collapsible panel at the top of the workspace generates candidate words from the language’s phonemes and syllable structure. Set a syllable range and a count, generate, then tick which candidates to keep (each is editable inline) and add them as new words. If nothing fits, it tells you to check the inventory and syllable structure or widen the range.

The Unassigned bucket

Words whose language can’t be determined (no langId, or one pointing at a deleted language) collect in an Unassigned bucket, reachable from the language selector. The migration from the old Language / Glossary template produces these for multi-language and zero-language worlds. Each unassigned word has a Move to language dropdown so you can file it under the right language, or you can delete it.

Glossopoeia · Orthography

Writing systems

Orthography is text-only in this version: you describe scripts and glyphs in words, not with custom glyph images or fonts. A collapsible writing-overview note sits at the top, then three tabs.

  • Writing Systems

    Each script has a Type (alphabet, abjad, abugida, syllabary, logographic, featural, mixed, or Other), Status, Direction, Word Separation, Letter Case, Numerals, Sentence End, Punctuation, and Notes. Writing System registers as a Concordance node.

  • Graphemes

    Each sign has a Belongs To reference to its script, a Role (consonant letter, vowel letter, diacritic, digraph, ligature, numeral, punctuation, logogram, or Other), a Romanization, a Spells reference to the Phoneme(s) it represents, a Shape Note describing the sign in words, and Notes.

  • Spelling Rules

    Each rule names the Sounds it’s about (references to Phonemes), how they’re Written As, the context (When), an Example, and Notes.

Glossopoeia · Corpus

Glossed example texts

Corpus holds Leipzig-style interlinear examples: one glossed example per entry. Each Example has a Source / Text (shared across the lines of one text), a Line / Order number for sequencing a multi-line text, a Genre / Type, the Interlinear grid (one column per word, rows for the object line, gloss, and any other tiers; cells can reference Lexemes), a Free Translation, an optional Literal Gloss, and Notes.

Gloss a sentence

A collapsible panel at the top auto-builds an interlinear for you. Paste a sentence in the language and it creates a new Example with Word, Parts, and Gloss rows pre-filled from your lexicon and any prior glosses. It’s a starting point: fix whatever it missed in the new Example.

Import & Export

Moving Cartulary data around

There's no account and no cloud here; your world is a file on your own disk. So sharing is just exporting a file and handing it over, and importing is the reverse. This page is the full map of what can travel and in what shape: a whole world, a single template, a pack of them, a constructed language, a stemma, a calendar, a pack of map stamps, a single map as an image, a story bible, and the smaller copies: one entry, a Linea tree, a Chronometric chart. The JSON is always the real backup; everything else is a copy for reading, not a save.

The whole world

The world-level exports live in World settings, except the HTML one, which is on the home screen.

  • Export .json

    The canonical save format, and the only world file you can import back. This is your backup; keep it next to your image folder. Everything else on this list is a one-way copy.

  • Export .md / .md (with secrets)

    A human-readable Markdown copy with every reference resolved to names. Good as a player handout, a printed reference, or context for an AI assistant. Not a save file. The plain version leaves secret-marked fields out; the with-secrets version keeps them, for your own eyes.

  • Export .zip (Markdown + images)

    The same Markdown, bundled with every referenced image in an images/ folder so the reference is self-contained. Also has a with-secrets variant.

  • Export world as HTML

    From the Export block on the home screen, a single self-contained HTML file that opens in any browser. Cross-references become anchor links, dates render through your calendar, and maps render as they do in the app, image, drawing, and pins together. Read-only, for sharing with people who don't run Cartulary.

  • Import .json

    Loads a previously exported world file. It replaces the current world, so export first if you want to keep what's open.

A single template

On any template, the Export button opens a menu with two formats: .template.json, the portable file another world can import, and .template.md, a readable reference copy. Import (the ↥ Import button under + New in the sidebar's Templates section) brings a .template.json into the current world. Built-in or custom, any template travels this way. Name collisions are handled below. The fuller treatment is on the Templates page.

A template pack

A pack is a named group of your own templates. Export pack writes the whole set to a .cartulary-pack.json; Import pack brings someone else's set in. Re-import the same pack later and Cartulary reconciles it template by template instead of duplicating, telling you what's new, what's unchanged, and what's changed since last time. Built-ins can't be packed, since packs are for templates you made or imported. See Templates for how packs sit in the sidebar.

A constructed language

In Glossopoeia, Export writes the selected language and all its data (sounds, words, rules, the lot) to a single .conlang.json. Import loads one as a new, independent language; any cross-language link that can't be resolved in the destination world is dropped, and the count is reported. Separately, Grammar .md and Grammar .html generate a readable reference grammar and dictionary. Those two are output only, not a re-importable save.

A stemma

A stemma, a rota or a tabula, travels as a single .stemma.json, exported from its row on the index or its canvas's top bar and brought back through Import on the index. A tabula's file is version 2 of the envelope, which a Cartulary from before boards refuses. Ids are regenerated on the way in so nothing collides, and any node or card linked to a record the destination world doesn't have arrives as an orphan to re-link, with a count in the import flash. Markdown, beside Export, writes the stemma as a .stemma.md for reading, a rota as a nested list and a tabula as lists under headings; it doesn't import back. Name collisions follow the rule below, and the details are on the Stemma page.

A calendar

A calendar exports to a .calendar.json for use in another world, and imports back. Entries bound to a calendar you replace will reckon against the new structure on the next load. Set them up in Kalendarium. Name collisions follow the rule below.

Stamps and brushes

Map stamps travel as packs: a .cartulary-stamps.json holding a pack's name, an id, and its stamps, each an svg body in a view box or a PNG tip. The Stamps panel at the top of Cartographia imports one (+ Import pack or brushes), and takes brushes by the same button: PNG files or a zip of them (a Wonderdraft folder's names and tint modes read), a Photoshop .abr, GIMP .gbr and .gih files, and Procreate .brush and .brushset files, each becoming a pack of tips. Export writes a pack back out, PNG zip writes it as PNGs in the Wonderdraft folder layout, and Export built-ins as a pack gives a correct file to start from. Every svg body is run through a sanitizer on the way in, and again on every load; a tip is decoded and re-encoded on import and only checked for shape on load. Packs don't collide by name; stamp ids are namespaced by the pack's id, so re-importing a pack replaces its own stamps in place and leaves every other pack alone. The formats and the allowlist are on the Stamps and brushes page.

A map as an image

Any saved map writes itself out as a standalone .svg or .png from the Download row above the canvas in the map's view: the drawing with the image and every painted layer inlined, stamps and tips resolved, hidden layers out, the map font embedded, and no pins. One-way, for use outside Cartulary; the details are on the Cartographia page.

A story bible

A story in Fabula, or a whole corpus, writes itself out as a bible: Export ↓ beside the title on a story's page or a corpus's opens a modal with the format, HTML or Markdown, and an Include secret-marked fields choice, off by default. The file is the story in its page's order, the Story's fields and panels, People, Plot, Outline with every scene's beats, Meaning, and Notes; a corpus's file has each of its stories in reading order. References to the world's entries become names, and in the HTML a reference to a record in the file is a link. Files are <name>.story.html, .story.md, .corpus.html, or .corpus.md; one-way, for reading. The world's own exports leave Fabula's records out, and the .json carries them.

Smaller copies

Three more one-way copies, each from where the thing lives. Export ↓ on an entry's page downloads that one entry as Markdown, named for its slug. Export tree as Markdown ↓ on Linea downloads the focal person's tree, numbered as the canvas numbers it. PNG and SVG in the Chronometric controls download the chart as you see it. None of them imports back.

When a name collides

Import a template, stemma, or calendar whose name already exists in the destination and Cartulary never overwrites silently. It stops and asks:

  • Skip

    Keep what's there, import nothing.

  • Replace

    Overwrite the existing one with the incoming copy. For a template, entries keep their link and any field data that no longer fits the new shape stays in place but hidden; for a stemma or calendar, the old contents are replaced outright.

  • Import as a new one named …

    Bring the incoming copy in under a name you type, so both survive.

Packs are the exception: a re-imported template pack reconciles per template rather than offering this three-way choice, and a stamp pack replaces its own stamps by id.

A read-only handout

Two ways to hand your world to someone who shouldn't edit it. Export the .json, open a fresh copy of the Cartulary .html file, import the .json there, and turn on Lock Mode: the reader browses everything but sees no edit controls. Lock Mode is friction, not security, since the toggle is two clicks away, so it stops honest readers rather than determined ones. For genuinely read-only sharing, the Markdown or HTML export carries no editing surface at all. Anything you marked secret drops out of every export you run without secrets.

JSON Structure

What's inside your .world.json

Every world you export lands in a single JSON file. The structure is designed to be human-readable and scriptable: if you want to bulk-edit entries, debug a corrupt save, or pipe your world data into another tool, this is the reference. Reverse-engineering from a known-good export works fine too, but this is the documented version.

Top-level shape

{
  "schemaVersion":  1,
  "world":          { ...world metadata, including the primary calendar },
  "templates":      [ ...template definitions ],
  "entries":        [ ...entry records ],
  "stemmas":        [ ...stemma documents ],
  "concordance":    { "positions": { ... } },
  "calendars":      [ ...non-primary calendars ],
  "celestialBodies":[ ...body definitions ],
  "ephemeris":      { ...Ephemeris view UI state },
  "glossopoeia":    { ...Glossopoeia data + UI state },
  "packs":          [ ...template packs ],
  "cartographia":   [ ...map records ],
  "mapStamps":      [ ...imported map stamps ],
  "fabula":         { ...Fabula's records and schemas },
  "viewPrefs":      { ...per-view UI preferences }
}

schemaVersion is currently always 1. A higher number in a file means it was written by a newer build; loading is refused with a recovery prompt. A lower or missing number is treated as 1.

Everything except schemaVersion, world, templates, and entries is optional. Older worlds without stemmas, celestialBodies, glossopoeia, cartographia, fabula, and so on load fine. Migration backfills each missing key to its empty default. New exports always include all keys.

Version-history snapshots are not part of the JSON file. They live in a separate IndexedDB store and don't survive clearing browser storage or moving the world to another machine. The exported file is the canonical save; snapshots are a session safety net only.

The world object

{
  "name":          string,
  "description":   string,
  "imageFolder":   string,           // suggested folder name for FSAA images
  "locked":        boolean,          // Lock Mode state
  "calendar":      { ...calendar },  // the primary calendar
  "currentMoment": { "year": number, "monthIndex": number, "day": number } | null,
  "createdAt":     ISO-8601 timestamp,
  "updatedAt":     ISO-8601 timestamp,
  "seededBuiltins":  [ string, ... ],
  "seededFields":    { [tplName]: [fieldLabel, ...] },
  "iconMigrations":  { [migrationKey]: true },
  "fieldRenames":    { [migrationKey]: true },
  "templateRenames": { [migrationKey]: true }
}

currentMoment is the world's "now" in primary calendar frame. It drives the time-cursor on the Kalendarium grid, the deceased-character obituary lines in Linea, and the time-aware pin filtering in Cartographia.

The migration-tracking maps (seededBuiltins, seededFields, etc.) prevent re-seeding work that's already been done: built-in templates the user deleted on purpose, fields renamed by upgrades, and so on. Safe to omit when constructing JSON from scratch; migration populates them on first load.

The calendar object

Used both for the primary calendar at world.calendar and for each entry in the top-level calendars array.

{
  "name":       string,
  "months":     [
    { "name": string, "days": number, "abbreviation": string },
    ...
  ],
  "eras":       [ { "name": string, "shortName": string, "startYear": number }, ... ],
  "notes":      string,
  "week": {
    "daysPerWeek": number,        // positive integer
    "dayNames":    [ string, ... ] // either empty (no header row) or exactly daysPerWeek strings
  },
  "celestials": [ string, ... ],  // moon-body id refs into top-level "celestialBodies"
  "seasons":    [
    {
      "id":       string,          // e.g. "sea-{4-char-suffix}"
      "name":     string,
      "startDay": number,          // 1-indexed day-of-year
      "endDay":   number,          // 1-indexed; endDay < startDay = wraparound through year-end
      "color":    string           // hex
    },
    ...
  ],
  "dateFormat": "name-day" | "abbr-day-pad" | "abbr-day" | "name-month" | "abbr-year-pad",
  "anchor":     { "absoluteDay": number }
}

A few things to know:

Month abbreviation is a v1.5 field, up to 3 characters. Used by the abbreviated date presets (abbr-day, abbr-day-pad, abbr-year-pad). If absent or empty, Cartulary derives a default from the first three letters of the month name.

dateFormat controls how calendar-date fields render across the world. The five preset IDs map to: name-day = "3 January, 2026"; abbr-day-pad = "03 JAN 2026"; abbr-day = "3 JAN 2026"; name-month = "January 3, 2026"; abbr-year-pad = "2026 JAN 03". Defaults to name-day if absent.

monthIndex in date values is 0-based into this array. Reordering or deleting a month invalidates existing dates that reference it. The in-app editor warns before doing this and walks all affected date surfaces.

celestials holds string id refs into celestialBodies (kind moon), not inline cycle data. Pre-v1.5 worlds stored inline objects here; migration moved those into celestialBodies and rewrote this field to refs. Cap is 5 per calendar.

Season wraparound: endDay < startDay means the season crosses year-end (e.g. a winter season with startDay 335 and endDay 59). Overlapping seasons are allowed; first-defined wins at a given day cell.

anchor is required on non-primary calendars. It's the absoluteDay (in primary frame) on which this calendar's year 1, month 1, day 1 falls. The primary calendar has no anchor; it defines the absolute frame. AbsoluteDay 0 = primary Y1M1D1.

The calendars array

Non-primary calendars. Empty array on single-calendar worlds. Each entry has the same shape as the calendar object above, plus a required anchor and a unique id:

[
  {
    "id":     string,    // e.g. "cal-{4-char-suffix}"
    "anchor": { "absoluteDay": number },
    ...all other calendar fields
  },
  ...
]

Entries bind to a non-primary calendar via a calendarId field. When set, all of that entry's date-bearing fields reckon in that calendar. When null or absent, dates reckon in primary.

Calendars export and import through a separate cartulary_calendar_export: 1 envelope. The collision modal on import allows Skip, Replace, or Rename; Replace is disabled when colliding with primary (a primary swap would re-frame every entry's stored dates).

Stars, planets, moons, and other bodies defined in Ephemeris. Empty array on worlds with no Ephemeris data.

    "orbitalElements": {
      "semiMajorAxis":        number | null,   // AU

The orbitalElements block appears on all bodies for schema consistency but is only meaningful on computable kinds. Narrative-kind bodies ignore it entirely.

The packs array

Named reference-sets over templates. Empty array if none. Added in v1.5.

[
  {
    "id":          string,
    "name":        string,
    "description": string,
    "templateIds": [ string, ... ]   // references templates[].id; built-ins are never members
  },
  ...
]

A pack groups templates by id for display and sharing. It never owns or copies them. Packs export and import through a separate cartulary_pack_export: 1 envelope, which carries the full member template definitions. On import, each member template gets a world id namespaced under the pack id (form: packId__originalId), so same-named templates from different packs coexist without collision.

The templates array

[
  {
    "id":     string,              // e.g. "tpl-character-a3f2"
    "name":   string,
    "icon":   string,              // single character, e.g. "◈"
    "color":  string,              // hex
    "kind":   "standard",
    "fields": [ ...field definitions ],
    "fabulaRole": "threads" | "themes"   // v1.7, optional: the world's Plot Thread or Theme / Motif template, which Fabula keeps
  },
  ...
]

fabulaRole marks the one template the world keeps for each of Fabula's two: "threads" on its Plot Thread, "themes" on its Theme / Motif. Migration sets it on every load where it's missing (the built-in first, then a rename of it, then a template of yours with the exact name that no pack holds) and clears a duplicate, so a hand-written file can leave it out. A flagged template stays an ordinary template with ordinary entries; it just lists and edits from Fabula's index instead of the sidebar (see Fabula).

kind is always "standard". Through v1.5 a template could be kind: "map", which put a mapData object on its entries; since v1.6 maps are their own records in the top-level cartographia array (below), and a v1.5 file's map-kind templates and entries are migrated into it on load.

Template IDs must be unique across the world. Field IDs must be unique within each template (including subfields recursively). Both are validated on load and import; a collision is rejected with a specific error rather than silently clobbered.

Field definitions

Each object in templates[].fields:

{
  "id":        string,             // e.g. "fld-description-71b4"
  "label":     string,
  "type":      "text" | "textarea" | "tags" | "select" | "multiselect" |
               "number" | "date" | "calendar-date" | "calendar-date-range" |
               "url" | "color" | "image" | "entry-ref" | "name-history" |
               "group" | "longform" | "grid",
  "help":      string,             // optional; shown when the field's label is hovered
  "required":  boolean,            // optional; default false. The form won't save while it's empty
  "secret":    boolean,            // optional; default false

  // type-specific:
  "options":         [ string, ... ],      // "select" / "multiselect": the choices, "Other" among them when the next key is on
  "otherTextField":  boolean,              // "select" / "multiselect": picking Other opens a text box for a choice of your own
  "dependsOn":       string,               // "select": the id of a select beside it whose value picks this one's choices
  "optionsByParent": { [parentChoice]: [ string, ... ] },   // "select", with dependsOn: the choices offered for each value of the parent
  "refTemplates":    [ string, ... ],      // "entry-ref": the target templates by NAME; a registered source's name counts
                                           //   ("Astronomical Body", "Map", "Cast (Fabula)")
  "refTemplatesId":  [ string, ... ],      // "entry-ref": the same targets by id, written at load from the names and by the builder
  "refTemplate":     string,               // "entry-ref", one target: the same pair in the singular
  "refTemplateId":   string,
  "refFilters":      { [templateName]: { "fieldId": string, "allowedValues": [ string, ... ] } },
                                           // "entry-ref": of that template, offer only the entries whose field holds one of the values
  "multi":           boolean,              // "entry-ref": single vs multi-select
  "relationKind":    "parent" | "spouse",  // "entry-ref": drives Linea and Chronometric's Marriages lane
  "subfields":       [ ...field definitions ],   // "group": nestable up to 3 levels deep

  // any type:
  "showWhen":        { "fieldId": string, "values": [ string, ... ] },
                                           // shown only while that field (same template, or same group) holds one of the values
  "halfWidth":       boolean               // legacy; left on some built-in fields and ignored since v1.3
}

Targets and parents come in two spellings, by name and by id, because field ids are generated per world while a built-in template or a pack file has to name its targets before the world exists. The authored form is the name: refTemplate or refTemplates (template names, case-sensitive), a filter's fieldLabel in place of its fieldId, a cascade's dependsOnLabel in place of dependsOn, and a condition's fieldLabel in showWhen. resolveTemplateRefs resolves each to its id when the world loads (refTemplateId, refTemplatesId, fieldId, dependsOn), and since v1.7 saving a template writes the names back from the ids, so the two stay in step. In a file you write by hand, give the names and let the ids follow; a name that resolves to nothing is kept, so a target restored later is targeted again, and a filter whose field can't be found falls back to no filter.

A cascade (dependsOn) and a condition (showWhen) reach only fields at their own level: a top-level field names a top-level field, a subfield names a sibling in the same group. A condition on a multi-select parent is met when any of the parent's picks is among values; one whose parent can't be found stays visible rather than hide data. A select with otherTextField stores { value, customText } once Other is picked, and a multi-select stores the typed answers beside the picked options, as the value table below shows.

Groups nest up to 3 levels deep (group→group→group); level-3 subfields cannot themselves be groups. Grid fields are top-level only and cannot appear as subfields inside a group.

The entries array

[
  {
    "id":         string,            // e.g. "ent-aria-a3f2"
    "templateId": string,            // references templates[].id
    "name":       string,
    "tags":       [ string, ... ],
    "fields":     { [fieldId]: value },
    "calendarId": string | null,     // bind to a non-primary calendar; null/absent = primary
    "createdAt":  ISO-8601 timestamp,
    "updatedAt":  ISO-8601 timestamp
  },
  ...
]

calendarId binds all date-bearing fields in that entry (calendar-date, calendar-date-range, name-history rows, spouse marriedOn/endedOn, and group subfields recursively) to the named non-primary calendar. Per-field calendar overrides aren't supported; it's all-or-nothing at the entry level.

Entry IDs must be unique across the world. Every entry's templateId must reference an existing template. Both are hard validation rules on load and import.

Map data

Each map record's mapData is the image, the pins, and the drawing:

{
  "image": string | null,          // filename or data URL, same shape as an image field
  "pins":  [
    {
      "id":        string,         // e.g. "pin-a3f2c1"
      "x":         number,         // 0..1, fraction of the canvas width
      "y":         number,         // 0..1, fraction of the canvas height
      "label":     string,
      "entryId":   string | null,  // optional cross-reference to another entry
      "color":     string | null,  // hex override; null = inherit default pin color
      "notes":     string,
      "permanent": boolean         // optional; default false. true = always visible in time mode
    },
    ...
  ],
  "drawing": {                     // the drawing under the pins
    "width":  number,              // canvas units; with an image, its natural pixel size
    "height": number,
    "background": { "fill": hex, "grain": number },   // grain 0..1; not drawn under an image
    "layers": [
      { "id": string, "name": string, "visible": boolean, "locked": boolean, "kind": "vector", "shapes": [ ...shapes ] },
      ...
    ]
  }
}

A raster layer (painted with the Brush or filled by Load image) holds one bitmap instead of shapes:

{
  "id":      string,
  "name":    string,               // "Paint" when the Brush made it
  "visible": boolean,
  "locked":  boolean,
  "kind":    "raster",
  "image":   string | null,        // a filename in the image folder ("<map id>-<layer id>.png") or a PNG, JPEG, or WebP data URL; null when empty
  "width":   number,               // the canvas units the bitmap covers; a canvas resize clips it
  "height":  number,
  "shapes":  []
}

Each shape carries id, type (rect, ellipse, polygon, stroke, region, text, or stamp), fill and stroke (hex or null), and strokeWidth (1 to 64), then by type: x, y, w, h for rect, ellipse, and stamp; points ([[x, y], ...]) for polygon and stroke; rings ([[[x, y], ...], ...], drawn even-odd, so a ring inside another is a hole) for region, which the Fill tool makes; smooth for rect, polygon, stroke, and region; arrow (none, end, or both, the arrowheads) for stroke; text, fontSize (24, 36, or 56), rotation, and arc (degrees, −180..180) for text; stampId and rotation for stamp. Layers draw in array order, so the last one is on top; a layer without kind is vector. Pins are not part of the drawing.

Pin coordinates are fractions of the canvas (0..1), so they stay positioned correctly at any display size. The canvas size shown in the UI (sizePct) and the zoom are session UI state and not persisted. A malformed drawing is coerced to defaults on load (one layer, a vellum background, the default 1600 × 1000 size) rather than refused; a shape that can't be rendered is dropped, and a raster layer whose image fails the check loads empty.

Unlinked pins (no entryId) are always visible in time mode. Linked pins marked permanent are also always visible, regardless of whether the linked entry has date data. Linked pins without permanent and without date data on the linked entry are hidden in time mode.

The fabula object

Fabula's stories, since v1.7. Every key is present on a new export, each array empty when the world has none; an older file without the object loads fine, and migration backfills it.

{
  "corpora":       [ ...corpus records ],
  "stories":       [ ...story records ],
  "cast":          [ ...cast records ],
  "arcs":          [ ...arc records ],
  "relationships": [ ...relationship records ],
  "plotPoints":    [ ...plot point records ],
  "promises":      [ ...promise and payoff records ],
  "parts":         [ ...part / act records ],

  "research":      [ ...research note records ],

The kinds, as schemas keys them and as each record's id names them: corpus, story, cast, arc, relationship, plotPoint, promise, part, chapter, scene, and research. Fabula's records aren't entries: nothing in entries or templates points at them, and they don't appear in the world's HTML or Markdown exports or in cartulary_entries_export files. A schema's field definitions take the same keys as a template's (the block above: id, label, type, help, required, secret, options, otherTextField, multi, subfields, showWhen, refTemplates with refTemplatesId), plus: core, true on a field Fabula reads, which the builder locks in type and place; reads, what reads a core field, the sentence the tables below quote; optionsLocked, true where a core select's options can't be edited; optionsFrom: "structure", on a plot point's Slot, whose options are the story's structure's slots; and panel, which of the Story's four panels (premise, genre, narration, setting) a field sits in. The stemmas array

Each stemma is a document in one of two layouts: a rota, a radial tree stored as a recursive node structure, or, since v1.7, a tabula, a board of placed items. The document:

{
  "id":          string,           // e.g. "stem-{time}-{6-char}"
  "name":        string,
  "description": string,
  "tags":        [ string, ... ],
  "layout":      "tabula",         // v1.7; a rota has none, so nothing migrates
  "root":        { ...a rota node, or a tabula's board node },
  "createdAt":   ISO-8601 timestamp,
  "updatedAt":   ISO-8601 timestamp
}

A rota node is one of two types:

// Freeform node (your own label, not tied to any record)
{
  "id":       string,              // e.g. "n-{time}-{6-char}"
  "slot":     number,              // 0..15 (sixteen radial slots, clockwise from 12 o'clock)
  "type":     "freeform",
  "label":    string,
  "color":    string,              // hex
  "icon":     string,              // single character or symbol
  "children": [ ...rota nodes ]
}

// Linked node (points at a record)
{
  "id":          string,
  "slot":        number,
  "type":        "linked",
  "entryId":     string,           // an entry's id, or a registered record's: a body, a map,
                                   //   a Glossopoeia or Fabula record, another stemma
  "_cachedName": string,           // the record's name when linked; shown once it is gone
  "children":    [ ...rota nodes ]
}

The root node has slot: 0 by convention. Child slots are unique within their parent's children (the editor enforces this; an import skips a duplicate slot). Nesting is unbounded.

A tabula's root is a board node, holding items placed by position, in z-order, and the board's lines:

{
  "id":     string,
  "type":   "board",
  "title":  string,
  "color":  string,                // hex
  "icon":   string,
  "items":  [ ...placed items ],   // drawn in array order, the last on top
  "shapes": [ ...lines ]
}

An item is a note, a linked card, an image, a column, or a board. Placed on a board it carries x, y, and w (integers, canvas pixels at 100%; heights are never stored); inside a column it carries none of them, though a lane may keep a w of its own:

// a note; the body is Markdown
{ "id": string, "type": "note",   "title": string, "body": string, "color": hex }
// a linked card, as the rota's linked node
{ "id": string, "type": "linked", "entryId": string, "_cachedName": string }
// an image: a filename in the images folder ("<stemma id>-<item id>.<ext>") or a data URL
{ "id": string, "type": "image",  "image": string, "caption": string }
// a column; flow only on a row, absent on a stack
{ "id": string, "type": "column", "title": string, "color": hex, "items": [ ... ],
  "flow": "row" }
// a sub-board, shaped as above, with its own items and shapes
{ "id": string, "type": "board",  ... }

A column holds cards or lanes, never both: its items are cards, or columns laid side by side (lanes), at any depth. A lane, or a stack under a card, may carry w alone, its width; the last lane takes what's left. Any card but a column or a board may carry items too: columns, its stacks, drawn under it; a card with none has no items key. A line in shapes is the map editor's stroke (id, type: "stroke", points, fill (null), stroke, strokeWidth, smooth, arrow; see Map data) plus anchors, { "start": item id | null, "end": item id | null }, the cards its ends stick to on this board. On load a tabula is coerced, never refused: a card loose among lanes goes into the first lane, a stray value lands on a default, and an item of a type the build doesn't know is kept and not drawn.

A stemma's own export wraps the document: { "cartulary_stemma_export": 1 | 2, "exported_at": timestamp, "stemma": {...} }, 1 for a rota and 2 for a tabula. A build without boards refuses envelope 2, and a build refuses any envelope newer than the ones it knows. A tabula's pan and zoom, per board, is kept in the browser with the appearance preferences and isn't part of the save file.

The concordance object

{
  "positions": {
    [entryId]: { "x": number, "y": number },
    ...
  }
}

Coordinates are in the force-directed graph's local space, not screen pixels. Zoom and pan are session UI state and not persisted. Entries without a saved position get auto-placed by force layout when first rendered, then their settled positions write back to this map on next save. Stale positions (whose entryId no longer exists) are scrubbed on load.

The viewPrefs object

{
  "timelineZoomLevel":       "year" | "month" | "day",
  "mapCalendarId":           string | null,   // Cartographia
  "timelineCalendarId":      string | null,   // Timelines · Vertical
  "chronometricsCalendarId": string | null,   // Timelines · Chronometric
  "familyCalendarId":        string | null,   // Linea
  "kalendariumGridYear": {                     // map, keyed by calendar rowKey
    "__primary__":  number,                    // the primary calendar
    "<calendarId>": number               // a non-primary calendar by id
  }
}

viewPrefs collects per-world UI preferences that persist with the world file rather than living in browser storage. The four *CalendarId keys hold the per-view calendar selector, one each for Cartographia, the Vertical timeline, the Chronometric timeline, and Linea; null means the primary calendar. kalendariumGridYear is a map remembering the last-viewed year per calendar grid, keyed by __primary__ for the primary calendar or a non-primary calendar's id. Every key is safe to omit; individual views read with null defaults.

Validation on load and import

Cartulary checks a set of hard rules before accepting a file. A file failing any of these is rejected with a specific error rather than partially loaded:

  • schemaVersion must not exceed the current build's schema version
  • world, templates (array), and entries (array) must all be present
  • Template IDs must be unique across the world
  • Field IDs must be unique within each template, including subfields recursively
  • Entry IDs must be unique across the world
  • Every entry's templateId must reference an existing template

A partial load on a failed validation would produce silent data corruption. Rejection with an error is intentional.

Migration

When Cartulary loads a file written by an older build, the migrate() function runs before any rendering. It:

  • Backfills new built-in templates that didn't exist when the world was created, unless their name is in seededBuiltins (meaning the user previously deleted them on purpose)
  • Backfills new fields on existing built-in templates via the same opted-out-deletion logic
  • Renames built-in templates and fields when the rename tables specify it, keeping IDs stable so existing entries survive the rename
  • Normalizes legacy entry-ref values from bare ID strings to the { id, label?, status? } object shape
  • Strips self-references (an entry can't reference itself in an entry-ref field)
  • Migrates any pre-v1.5 inline calendar moon data into celestialBodies and rewrites the calendar's celestials array to id refs
  • Moves the entries of any v1.5 map-kind template into the cartographia array and drops the map-kind templates
  • Backfills mapData.drawing on every map record and re-sanitizes every stamp body in mapStamps
  • Flags the world's Plot Thread and Theme / Motif templates fabulaRole where neither is flagged, and clears a duplicate flag (v1.7)
  • Backfills fabula with an empty array for each kind and the seeded schema for each, replaces an unedited schema whose seed is behind the build's, and brings an edited schema's core fields up to the seed (v1.7)
  • Coerces every tabula document in stemmas to its shape, on every load (v1.7); a rota is left as it is

Migration operates on a defensive clone of the input. Your original file is never written through during this step.

Colophon

How this was made, and on whose terms

A colophon is the note at the back of a book that says how it was made: the type, the press, the hands. This is that note, for both the tool and this guide. None of it is marketing. It's the production record, plus the few commitments that aren't really commitments because they fall out of the build.

The build

Cartulary is one HTML file. The program, the interface, and the fonts are all inlined into that single document, so once it's on your drive it runs with nothing else: no server, no install, no network. The app is built with React, draws its graph and timeline views with d3, and keeps your working world in your browser's own IndexedDB. Exports are plain .json. There is no backend anywhere in the picture, because there's nothing for a backend to do.

This documentation site is hand-authored, separate from the app, and built the same way: a single file, fonts inlined, no external requests after it loads. It's a small client-side router that swaps panels by URL hash, so the back and forward buttons work the way you'd expect.

Accessibility, honestly

An honest statement, not a badge. A good amount of Cartulary works from the keyboard: Escape closes panels and pickers, Enter and clicking away commit edits, Ctrl or Cmd plus scroll zooms the timeline and graph views, and date fields have stepper buttons and typed inputs. Some controls carry ARIA labels, and images use whatever alt text you give them.

What doesn't work yet is worth stating plainly. The canvas-based views, the Concordance graph, the Chronometric chart, Fabula's timeline, the Linea family tree, Stemma's rotas and tabulas, and the Cartographia map, are built for a sighted user with a pointer; they aren't navigable by a screen reader. Two are partial exceptions. The map editor around its canvas has one key per tool, arrow keys to nudge, named swatches, and a live region that names the selection, but a shape or a stroke still has to be placed with a mouse, a pen, or a finger. Stemma's canvases have keys of their own: a rota is walked, drilled, reordered, and zoomed from the keyboard, and a tabula's selection is nudged, copied, pasted, and deleted, but a card is placed, a line drawn, and a tabula panned with a pointer. There's been no formal screen-reader pass and no WCAG audit. The interface has two dark themes, the default and a High Contrast one, and no light mode; it doesn't follow your system color-scheme setting. The app doesn't gate its animations on a reduced-motion preference, though this documentation site does. If you depend on accessibility features to work at all, go in knowing those gaps are real.

Typefaces

Two families, nine faces, all inlined. Headings and the display text are set in Cormorant Garamond; body copy, labels, and interface text are DM Sans. Both are open-licensed and travel inside the file, so the type renders the same offline, on a machine that has never seen them installed, ten years from now.
